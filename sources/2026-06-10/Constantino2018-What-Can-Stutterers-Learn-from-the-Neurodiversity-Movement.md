# What Can Stutterers Learn from the Neurodiversity Movement?

Christopher D. Constantino, Ph.D., CCC-SLP1 

## ABSTRACT

Neurodiversity is both an empowerment movement and a way of thinking about disability. Rather than focusing on pathology and impairment, neurodiversity emphasizes natural variation and the unique skills, experiences, and traits of neurodivergent individuals. People who stutter are beginning to work with and derive value from these concepts. In this article, we look at the history of neurodiversity and its key ideas. We discuss the conventional view of disability, the medical model, which situates disability within the individual as pathology. We also take up social and relation models of disability, which situate disability in social oppression or mismatches between individuals and their environment. Neurodiversity has not been without controversy. We look at some of the disagreements surrounding issues of intervention and cure. The ideas of neurodiversity are applied to stuttering, and a case example illustrating therapy using these ideas is given. We conclude that therapy should focus on subject’s well-being and not normalization of superficial behaviors. 

KEYWORDS: Stuttering, neurodiversity, social model of disability, identity-first, stutterer 

Learning Outcomes: As a result of this activity, the reader will be able to (1) discuss the neurodiversity movement and how it relates to stuttering; (2) describe the difference between the medical and social models of disability; and (3) implement therapy for stuttering that is sensitive to neurodiversity issues. 

The political landscape has changed dramatically over the past couple of decades. Recent technologies, such as the internet and mobile social media applications, have connected people across the world. The exchange of ideas and dialogue enabled by these technologies has been a catalyst for the rapid growth of new empowerment movements. Disabled people,a especially with communication impairments like stuttering, have been one of the largest beneficiaries of these new modes of connecting. In the past, disabled people often lacked a community of others with similar experiences and were isolated by their difficulties communicating. One fruit of the increased communication among disabled people has been the neurodiversity movement. This article will examine the concept of neurodiversity, give definitions and a brief history, describe some of its key components, and apply the concept to stuttering. Finally, I will discuss some implications of neurodiversity for stuttering therapy by highlighting a case example from my own therapy experiences. 

## HISTORY, DEFINITIONS, AND TERMINOLOGY

The term “neurodiversity” was first used by Australian sociologist Judy Singer, an autistic, to describe conditions like autism, dyslexia, and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in her 1998 sociology honors thesis.2,3 The term was popularized by journalist Harvey Blume in a 1998 Atlantic article about Wired magazine’s Web site and geek culture.4 The idea of neurodiversity has developed into a political and social movement. This movement was developed in the late 1990s and early 2000s by groups of autistic persons.5 It’s scope has grown and it is now associated with the struggle for civil rights of all those diagnosed with developmental neurological disorders, such as ADHD, bipolar disorder, developmental dyspraxia, dyslexia, epilepsy, Tourette’s syndrome, and even stuttering.6 

Neurodiversity was a deliberate attempt by Singer to shift the discussion of disabilities away from talk of deficits, disorders, and impairments. Singer was interested in liberation movements and activism, wanting to do for neurologically different people what feminism and gay rights had done for their respective populations. By echoing positive terms like biodiversity and cultural diversity, she highlighted the unique skills and advantages that come with different neurological ways of abnormal, or able-bodied and disabled, neurodiversity advocates talk of neurotypical and neurodivergent. Neurotypical people have the typical or majority neurology, while neurodivergent individuals possess a minority neurology or one that deviates from the norm.9 

## NATURAL VARIATION AND RIGHTS

Neurodiversity is the idea that variations in brain structure, function, and development should be appreciated and accepted as natural tion, neurodiversity advocates mean that a given percentage of any healthy population is expected to be neurodivergent.11 Furthermore, for this variation to be considered natural, it should not be caused by a random misfortune. Its continued propagation in the population should have some evolutionary explanation; that is, there may be some benefits to possessing the trait, even if they are not clear to us. A good example of this is sickle cell anemia.12 Having one allele of the gene produces increased resistance to malaria. The disease appears only when two alleles are present. The gene survives in the gene pool not because it begins as a new mutation each time but because having one allele improves the possessor’s chances of survival in areas with malaria.6 Neurodivergent conditions may be similar; it appears that both autism and schizophrenia alter the risk of cancer in individuals and their relatives.13,14 

The neurodiversity movement emphasizes the moral and political consequences of accepting neural differences as natural variation. In the most general sense, this calls for “social acceptance and equal opportunity for all individuals regardless of their neurology.”15 Differences in brain structure and function should have no more significance than differences in skin color and sex. Neurodiversity activists argue that protecting their rights is no different than protecting the rights of other ethnocultural minority groups. Group-specific rights for minorities are necessary to guarantee that all citizens are given equal treatment, and true equality is achieved only when difference is accommodated.11,16 

Neurodiversity advocates suggest that there is value to these variations and demand recognition, acceptance, and rights.6 Since these variations are valuable, rather than ameliorating the neurodiversity through medical interventions, the neurodivergent should be treated as a minority group in need of protection.11 Neurotypical people have a responsibility to protect the neurodivergent because they are at risk of being swallowed by the majority culture.6,16 In the same way that different races and ethnic groups add to cultural diversity, different neurological groups add to neurodiversity. The neurodiversity movement takes pride in and celebrates these differences. 

This celebratory discourse is in line with Singer’s original intent. She wanted to emphasize advantages and deemphasize disadvantages. For example, people with autism are often quite good at memorizing facts and highly intelligent in ways that are difficult to test on IQ tests. They are capable of intense focus for extended periods of time. Some have argued that it is possible to construct language of impairment in describing a neurotypical brain, compared with an autistic brain: Compared with autistic people, a neurotypical brain is easily distracted, obsessively social, and suffers from a deficit in attention to detail.4 

## CONTROVERSIES

Neurodiversity becomes controversial when discussing treatment, intervention, and cure. The tension is between viewing neurological variation as a deficit and just a difference. 17 Often, neurodiversity advocates promote interventions that support subjective well-being and improved quality of life.18,19 However, they will oppose interventions which aim to reduce or eliminate strange but harmless behaviors, such as self-stimulating behaviors in autism.5,20 Some members of the neurodiversity movement also oppose efforts to ameliorate or cure autism and other neurological differences, reasoning that if they are truly differences and not deficits then to cure them would be eugenic, similar to attempting to eliminate a particular race or cultur e.11,21 The resistance to seeking cures often puts the movement at odds with both parent support groups and researchers. 

Another reason the neurodiversity movement has remained controversial is that it makes moral claims, which positions opponents as not only wrong but unethical. This often pits them against parents of autistic children and other advocates. Certain forms of behavioral therapy are highly divisive within the neurodiversity movement, such as applied behavioral analysis. They see the therapy as focusing too narrowly on normalization of autistic behaviors at the expense of subjective well-being .18,20 Rather than improving the lives of autistic people, neurodiversity advocates argue that these interventions are aimed at making them appear more neurotypical.5,22 

An additional division is in the use of terms. The medical community and parent support groups have gravitated toward person-first language because of the emphasis it puts on the humanity of individuals with disorders. The development of person-first language (e.g., person with autism) was meant to avoid collapsing the disorder onto the individual. Person-first language expresses that individuals are, first of all, people and, thus, much more than their disorders.23 Their status as human beings is primary, and their status as disabled is secondary. Activism by members of the National Stuttering Project wishing to be called people who stutter instead of stutterers caused the practice to be adopted widely by speech-language pathologists. This was one of the first instances of person-first language being adopted officially by professionals.24 

Members of the neurodiversity movement attempts to cultivate pride in a minority group identity.5,6,17 They do not see themselves as first people and then disabled because they do not separate their disability from their humanity; rather, their disability is central to their humanity. Members of the movement promote association with their disabled identity to cultivate mutual support and community.17,18 Therefore, neurodiversity self-advocates tend to prefer identity-first language.20 Many in the neurodiversity movement prefer autistic persons, autists, or aspies rather than person with autism or person with Asperger’s syndrome.6 Similarly, there have been voices within the stuttering movement suggesting a move away from person who stutters and a return to stutterer. 25 

People from such diverse disability movements as blind, deaf, autistic, and stuttering have all spoken out against person-first language.25–27 Some find the language clumsy. The awkward phrasing of person-first language signals that stuttering is undesirable and not normal. Others believe that words such as blind and deaf already implicitly include the word person and that by explicitly stating the word, it portrays the disabled as a special, sensitive group in need of pity and assistance.27 Members of the neurodiversity movement believe that person-first language suggests that there is a normal person trapped behind the disability, indicating that the person is somehow separate from the disability.6 They offer the perspective that disabilities such as autism are, in fact, not separate from the person.1,2 8 Being autistic influences everything the person does, thinks, and feels; they would, therefore, be entirely different people if they did not have autism.29 Well-known autistic scientist and autobiographical author Temple Grandin articulates this view when she says, “If I could snap my fingers and be nonautistic, I would not. Autism is part of what I am.”30 

## MODELS OF DISABILITY

Neurodiversity represents a perspective shift, away from thinking of disability in medical terms and toward thinking about disability in social terms. Disability does not exist in a vacuum but as an interaction between the environment and the body. This perspective shift can be jarring when misunderstood. To facilitate an understanding of the neurodiversity perspective, it is important to have some background knowledge of different models of disability. 

## Medical Model

The medical and research community has put forth many psychological, psychoanalytical, biological, and neurological theories about the etiology of various neurodivergent conditions, like autism and stuttering. All share the assumption that neurodivergent people have a deficit in functioning that can, and should, be researched, identified, modified, and cured. This way of understanding disability is known as the medical model, which is the default way most of society thinks about disability today. This is the dominant model in medicine and research, and it categorizes bodies according to their functioning. The Diagnostic and Statistics Manual (DSM) is an example of medical model thinking.32 According to this model, individuals are disabled by their bodily deficits. Disability activist A. J. Withers defines the medical model this way: 

“A clinical approach to disability which focuses on the use of diagnostic tools to identify pathology and make interventions in that pathology to cure or minimize it.” Under this framework, disability is based in the body, normal is constructed as ideal, disabled people are dependent, and our identities are tragedies 

The medical model claims that the locus of disability is the individual’s body. There is a binary between normal or healthy bodies and deviant or abnormal bodies. Implicit in the medical model are the goals of eliminating and curing disability.34 Individuals with abnormal bodies are disabled by their bodies and should be healed. These goals put the medical model at odds with the neurodiversity movement. 

When no biological markers are present, the medical model establishes deficits based on behavioral deviations from average.35 For example, subtle neurological markers have only recently been identified in stuttering, but stuttering has long been classified as a disorder based on behavior.36,37 This type of classification often ignores advantageous behaviors, the cause of behaviors, and society’s role in deciding which behaviors are appropriate.38 Therefore, the classification does not distinguish among (1) conditions that are the result of societal discrimination, (2) those that represent poor fit between body and society, and (3) disease.22 The medical model’s focus on behavioral deviations and disadvantages again puts it at odds with the neurodiversity movement. 

The aim of the medical model, amelioration of disease, is not deliberately malevolent. Researchers, clinicians, and parents have charitable intentions. However, the neurodiversity movement believes there are negative consequences to looking at natural variation as disease. The medicalization of neurological conditions creates a taboo and stigma around them.39 Once a human trait is subject to the medical gaze, it becomes undesirable.40 This creates a stigma around the trait in the public consciousness.41 This stigma is then internalized by those with the trait.42,43 

Often the existence of public stigma is enough to make a condition appear pathological.6 Over a century ago, it was documented that homosexuality caused subjective distress and was added to the DSM in 1952 and treated as a disease.45 However, this apparent distress experienced by gay people was not inherent to homosexuality but was due to living in a society with strong prejudice against homosexuality. This did not only lead to active discrimination against gay people; it also encouraged them to pass as straight and hide their lifestyle and feelings. In a homophobic society many, if not most, gay people will appear pathological.6 Homosexuality was removed from the DSM in 1973 because it was argued that mental distress was a consequence of the condition, specifically society’s reaction to the condition, and not it’s etiology.46 Therefore, the cure for this mental distress was not medical treatment but wider acceptance of homosexuality. 

Autistic people and stutterers make similar arguments. They contend that their well-being will be more improved by social acceptance than medical intervention. There is an analogous phenomenon to “passing as straight” with stutterers—passing as fluent.47 Experiences of discrimination and delegitimization encourage stutterers to hide their identity.48 To say that autistic people and stutterers have a pathology because of the consequences of their neurological condition is to engage in victim blaming. These consequences are often the result of society’s reaction to them and not the conditions themselves. Much of the distress of autistic people and stutterers is the result of severe circumstances and a hostile society rather than anything having to do with their bodies and minds.6,46,49–51 Social models of disability move beyond the medical model by taking into account societal barriers to health and flourishing. 

## Social and Relational Models

The social model of disability makes a distinction between impairment and disability, in which impairment is a medical or physical condition and disability is a social condition.52 Disability is imposed on top of impairment by way of discrimination, stigma, and prejudice.53 This is easiest to picture when considering physical impairments; for example, a person with cerebral palsy may be impaired by the spasticity in their legs but be disabled by lack of access to an elevator or wheelchair ramp. It is subtler with stuttering, where the person is impaired by effortful speech and disabled by expectations of fluency, by discrimination against nonstandard speech, and by speaking time constraints. Contrary to the locus of disability in the medical model, disability in the social model is in society and its organization. People are disabled by society, not by their bodies.54 The social model suggests that society is just as responsible for enabling individuals with disabilities to be successful as it is in disabling them. Therefore, it is society’s responsibility to not only remove barriers but also implement accommodations. 

There are many other ways to model disability, from updated social models55— such as feminist materialist models56—to the relational model,57 the political model,58 the radical model,33 the cultural model,59,60 and the minority model.61 Models are never truth but ways to simplify complex phenomena to make them understandable. We use statistical models to fit our data because if we looked at the raw numbers it would be overwhelming and incomprehensible. Engineers use models to simplify physics when designing objects because they cannot account for every variable. Models of disability are no different; they are simply a complex phenomenon, so it can be understood. The different models mentioned here emphasize different variables involved in the disabled experience to create a particular understanding of disability. In their most basic forms, the problem in the medical model is a biological pathology and the problem in the social model is social oppression. However, more advanced forms of the models and new conceptions of disability often put the emphasis on a mismatch between the body of the disabled person and the social milieu in which the person exists. Disability is produced by this mismatch.57,58 

The neurodiversity movement situates itself within the more relationally oriented models, suggesting that bodies are neither healthy nor pathological but diverse and variable. Neurodiversity rejects the medical model’s claim that autism is a pathology and the social model’s claim that it is an impairment. Rather, there is a mismatch between the autistic and an environment not designed for them. Many have argued that stuttering fits this very same paradigm.62,63 The experience of stuttering in an environment designed for and accommodating of stuttering, such as at a FRIENDS or National Stuttering Association conference, is markedly different from the experience of stuttering in normal circumstances, where stuttered speech is not accommodated.64 

## CRITICISMS

Neurodiversity and the emphasis of the movement on fundamental differences are not without criticisms. Some of these are unique to autism. For example, it is not clear that all people with autism have a single shared difference—a neurological and genetic foundatio n65—although some researchers do argue that autism is entirely genetic.66 Insisting that there is a shared biological essence to autistic individuals might, in fact, worsen discrimination because it suggests a real biological difference between people with autism and everyone else. It is this insistence on real difference that is at the basis of most societal oppression, like racism and sexism (i.e., the belief that one race or sex is biologically inferior). Indeed, the goal of many liberation movements, from racial and ethnic minorities to women, was to show that they were not fundamentally different from the dominant group. The aim was to show that minorities and women were equal to white males. The claim of the neurodiversity movement that there is a fundamental difference represents a departure from past liberatory discourses. 

A false dichotomy is often created between researching cause and cure and working to increase quality of life. Research in neuroscience, medicine, and genetics is disparaged because activists increasingly demand the limited resources available for disabled interests be used to investigate educational, psychological, and social interventions that directly address the daily difficulties of living with disability. However, these are not mutually exclusive goals; investigations of the nervous system and genes underlying behaviors may open the way toward practical treatments for clinical symptoms. Furthermore, it is not clear that the funds are entirely transferable, and money denied to genetic research is not necessarily available for quality-of-life research. 

There is also a rift between, what is often called, high-functioning and low-functioning autism.67 It is controversial to divide autistic people by functioning. One argument against the distinction between high and low functioning is that these terms are relative. They are based on how well autistic people are able to mimic neurotypical behavior; high function is code for, “you don’t seem autistic.” The labels high- and low-functioning end up serving stereotypes already present in society rather than saying anything meaningful about the people thus labeled. 

Despite this controversy, the added nuance of distinguishing between levels of functioning does reconcile some of the difficulties inherent to neurodiversity. It is often high-functioning autistics that are leading the charge that autism should not be cured. Some low-functioning autistics agree that those with high-functioning autism should not be cured. However, they feel that their own low-functioning autism represents a profound handicap and they would like to be cured.67 Many researchers and writers in autism make this argument, using the distinction between high and low functioning to attempt to resolve some of the tension surrounding cures, deficits, and differences.6,17,67 

Thus, not all autistic people are won over to neurodiversity’s cause. For many people living with low-functioning autism and their caretakers, neurodiversity can feel empty. Autism can be violent, isolating, and disruptive.8 As Jonathan Mitchell, an autistic person who blogs against neurodiversity, says, “Most persons with an autism-spectrum disorder have never expressed their opinions on someone’s blog and never will. The neurodiverse often reach a vulnerable audience, as many persons on the spectrum have low self-esteem. Neurodiversity provides a tempting escape valve.”68 Many autistic people are incapable of the level of communication required to participate in the discussion. It is easy for those who are most able to speak to dominate the discussion; while the opinions of those unable to speak are left silent.67,68 

Autistic people who disagree with some of neurodiversity’s claims argue that not all the problems and complications that come with autism can be reconciled by neurodiversity and social models of disability. Some autistic people claim to have trouble making friends and finding romantic partners not because of discrimination but because they often have difficulty reading social cues.68 Others have pointed out that since they have a hard time staying on task and applying themselves, they have not been able to develop the skills necessary to compete in the job market. They have difficulty finding employment because their impairment limits their skills not because of social bias.67,68 Many disabilities, autism included, give individuals perceptual and motor impairments that can make employment difficult and limit leisure activities. 68 

Despite these challenges, people continue to take up and make use of the ideas in neurodiversity. Disability is always an interaction between the social environment and bodily structures. The challenges brought by the bodily structures themselves and the mismatch between body and environment do not necessarily make life less valuable or worthwhile. These neurodivergent ways of being and their corresponding subjective experiences are an equally valuable part of human diversity.11,17 Nonetheless it is necessary to recognize that acceptance and accommodation of disability will not ameliorate all deficits. Many stutterers and other disability rights advocates already acknowledge that deficits themselves lower quality of life.18,22 

## STUTTERING AND NEURODIVERSITY

How well does neurodiversity apply to the context of stuttering? Stuttering is certainly a natural variation rather than a disease. It is not caused by injury or random genetic mutations. It is well documented that there are heritable genetic influences that at least predispose a person to develop stuttering.69,70 Stuttering is highly heritable and adopted children are more likely to stutter if their biological parents stutter than if their adoptive parents stutter.71 Researchers estimate that 80% of stuttering is the result of genetic effects.69,72 

There are also levels of functioning in stuttering. Both low-functioning autistics and stutterers with struggled speech are disabled by their condition above and beyond the disabling effect of society.6,54 High-functioning autistics and stutterers with spontaneous speech can very often live successful and independent lives with very little disability, especially in accommodating social environments. However, there is a notable distinction between levels of functioning in autistic people and stutterers: severity of stuttering is not static. Not only is stuttering highly variable,73 but stuttering is responsive to therapy across the lifespan.74,75 It does not make sense to label someone a low- or highfunctioning stutterer due to stuttering’s dynamic nature. 

There are other differences between autism and stuttering. One is the pervasive effects of each. Autism is pervasive in a way that stuttering is not. There is no doubt that stuttering greatly affects one’s life.76 Every experience of spoken communication is potentially affected. The compounded effect of a lifetime of these experiences is profound.48–50,77–79 Stuttering has far ranging effects on personal relationships, vocational attainment, and financial success.49,50,77,80 But even the most profound stutterers experience fluency; in fact, a high percentage of their syllables is likely to be fluent.81,82 Therefore, it is possible for a stutterer to imagine speaking and living, separate from stuttering. Although these experiences may be fleeting, most stutterers have experienced life as a fluent person. 

The argument that to eliminate the stuttering would eliminate the person doing the stuttering is not as convincing as in the case of autism. While I certainly value my experiences as a stutterer and feel strongly that eliminating them would drastically change who I am today, there is a distinction between my identity and my stutter. Furthermore, my stutter has changed dramatically over the years. In many situations, I am highly fluent, yet I still feel fundamentally myself. 

As within the autistic community, there is a diversity of views among stutterers. Some stutterers struggle with difficulties associated with stuttering, viewing their difference as a lonely cure.84 Other stutterers, especially those active within the stuttering self-help and disability rights movements, celebrate their stuttering as inseparable from their identities.25 This is reflected in their embrace of the term “stutterers” rather than persons who stutter. They challenge efforts to find a cause and cure for stuttering.85 They argue that seeking a cure for stuttering aims at normalization, further stigmatizes the disorder, and has eugenic consequences. 11 

Despite these differences, there are experiences stutterers have in common with autistic people and lessons to be learned from neurodiversity. Neurodiversity advocates challenge us to think critically about the objectives of our therapies. One criticism of some autism therapies from a neurodiversity perspective is that they do not aim to relieve the suffering of the autistic person but to make autistic people appear more normal.18,20 In these therapies, the goal is not to maximize quality of life for the autistic person, but rather minimize the appearance of autism. This is strongly echoed in many fluency therapies, where the goal of therapy is fluency but not necessarily ease or spontaneity of speech.86 Many of our therapies attempt to maximize fluent speech at the expense of spontaneity, attention, and effort. An important takeaway from the neurodiversity movement is that, to quote Autistic Self Advocacy Network cofounder Ari Ne’eman and first openly autistic White House appointee, “trying to make someone ‘normal’ isn’t always the best way to improve their life.”7 Likewise, trying to make stutterers fluent is not always the best way to improve their lives. Developing therapy objectives that correspond with and embrace the stutterer’s identity while making speech easier and less effortful may better align with the priorities of the individual in therapy. 

## THE EFFECTS OF NEURODIVERSITY

Ultimately, the usefulness of neurodiversity lies in its effect on the neurodivergent. If quality of life is improved and neurodiversity helps people find meaning and joy in their experiences, then it is helpful. If the movement discourages neurodivergent individuals from seeking help and, therefore, it lessens their quality of life, then it is harmful. This is an empirical question and there is some research addressing it. 

Using an online survey, Kapp et al17 examined the perceived opposition between the medical model and the neurodiversity movement by evaluating the opinions of people with different relationships to autism, including autistic people, relatives and friends of autistic people, and people with no specified relationship to autism. The authors found that people who self-identified as autistic and were aware of neurodiversity were significantly more likely than those not aware of neurodiversity to view autism as a positive identity and not desire a cure. Nonetheless, when asked how autism should be treated, there was a large amount of overlap between groups. All groups recognized the negative aspects of autism and endorsed parenting practices that both celebrate autism and mitigate some of these negative aspects. Importantly, those who were aware of the neurodiversity movement endorsed more positive emotions about autism than those who were not. The opposite was not true. There was no difference between autistic people familiar with neurodiversity, relatives of autistic people, and people with no specified relation to autism in endorsing the negative effects of autism. This suggests that autistic people who are familiar with neurodiversity are nuanced in their thinking about it. They can recognize the strengths of autism without obscuring an understanding of the difficulties that come with it.17 

This openness to both the positives and negatives of neurodivergent experiences suggests that those exposed to the neurodiversity movement are more likely to transcend the false dichotomy between ability and disability.19 They may be more able to harness their neurodivergent traits in developmentally beneficial ways, while still working on their deficits. Neurodiversity helps people find meaning and joy in their disabled experiences without causing them to overlook the negatives.17,19 Such a nuanced view is consistent with the neurodiversity movement’s position that neurodivergent conditions, while having value, also come with vulnerabilities that require protection.87 Neurodiversity advocates argue that there is value in their stuttering,88 but that accommodation and therapy may still be necessary because stuttering also makes them vulnerable to negative experiences, such as effortful speech and discrimination.49,83 

Much of the disability that accompanies stuttering is the result of social forces. Stutterers experience active discrimination.89–92 They are stigmatized and stereotyped by society and are often falsely characterized as shy, self-conscious, and anxious people who lack confidence.51 They are also paid less than their fluent peer s.49,78 Nonetheless, it is far from obvious that stuttering will cease to be disabling if society accepts stuttering as valuable. People will still have difficulty communicating with others93–95 and experience the frustration of not being able to express themselves as they would like.79,83 

## CASE EXAMPLE

Next, I present an example of how I use the ideas found in the neurodiversity movement in therapy. There are two key ideas from neurodiversity that I emphasize. The first is the goal of any therapy should not be normalization of speech for its own sake but improving subjective well-being. Therefore, ease of speech is valued over fluency. The second is that being a person who stutters is no more shameful or embarrassing than being any other kind of minority. 

Mocking of stuttering is a form of discrimination. Stutterers have the right to stutter and to demand accommodations for their speech. 

This case example is based on my work as a speech–language pathologist at a middle school. I had a large caseload of young stutterers who I saw in several small groups. The students coming from elementary school, with whom I had not worked with previously, were mostly coming from prolonged speech programs, where they practiced a reduced speaking rate, continuous phonation, light articulatory contacts, and easy onsets. They were taught if they did not want to stutter, they needed to speak using these techniques. These students were often capable of elevated levels of fluency in the therapy room. However, they avoided speaking when in the classroom and said that their techniques were very difficult to use outside of therapy. They did not know how to handle stutters when their techniques failed them. 

We started therapy by discussing why they were there. They said they were in therapy because they stuttered. I asked them why being a person who stuttered would land them in therapy. They had many answers: because stuttering made it hard to talk, because stuttering made them talk funny, because their parents did not want them to stutter, because they were made fun of for stuttering, because stuttering was bad. We discussed each of these in turn, eventually concluding that stuttering was not their fault, that being made fun of for stuttering was discrimination, and that expecting them to not stutter was expecting them not to be themselves. They said these conversations were empowering, and they were excited by these new and challenging ideas. At this point, several therapy sessions into the semester, most of my students were on board to learn how to become better stutterers. 

Many of the students had very convoluted, struggled stuttering patterns. They were hesitant to stutter out loud without mitigating the stutter with an avoidance strategy. They would use interjections, restart sentences and phrases, speak around feared words, substitute words, and generally do anything they could to avoid stuttering. It was nearly impossible to work on stuttering more easily because they were not letting themselves stutter to begin with. It was difficult to distill their stuttering from all the other behaviors they were doing. They were trying their best to be fluent speakers and it was only making speaking harder. All their efforts to be fluent only worsened their subjective wellbeing. 

My rule for the therapy room was that we had to try to stutter as much as possible while inside. This was hard for them to accept at first. We did a lot of desensitization activities to get comfortable stuttering. We took turns mimicking each other’s stutters. Sometimes this would turn into a contest where we would stutter like one another and the others had to guess who we were mimicking. This got them thinking deeply and critically about what they were doing while they were stuttering and what the others were doing as well. They would comment, “I like how John stutters, I’m going to try and stutter more like John,” or “Do I really make that sound when I stutter? I am going to try to stop doing that.” They were encouraged to play with and modify their stuttering. 

We took turns calling local stores and giving directions to each other on how we should stutter. While the speaker was on the phone, his or her partner would touch distinct parts of the speaker’s arm. Different spots on their arm corresponded to different kinds of stutters, for example, the elbow might be a prolongation and the shoulder a block. The speaker had to keep stuttering until their partner released their arm. The students had a lot of fun making each other stutter for longer and longer amounts of time. Rest assured, if they felt they were made to stutter for an unfair amount of time, they would get their partner back when the roles were reversed. This eventually led to games of one-upmanship, where the students tried to see who could stutter the most in the phone calls. 

Once they were letting themselves stutter, they could begin to work on stuttering more easily, with less tension, and less struggle behaviors. Their self-efficacy would immediately improve. We could do assignments, like calling up a pizza parlor and asking for the price of a large pie, where they would have to tell me ahead of time which behaviors they would work on. For example, one student might have the goal of not using any starter sounds when stuttering, another may have a goal of not restarting their sentences in response to a moment of stuttering, while another may be trying to eliminate tongue protrusion while stuttering. The more comfortable they were stuttering, the more they were able to complete their goals in harder and harder situations. We never made fluency a goal nor talked about our stuttering in terms of fluency. Instead we tried to be the best stutterers we could be. 

We also talked about the value of stuttering and the good that might come from it. We could talk about this because we were not aiming for fluency. Many people who stutter have pointed out that therapists often contradict themselves by discussing acceptance but then working on fluency. There is no conflict between valuing stuttering and working to stutter with less effort. We discussed what, if anything, we gain from stuttering and what we would miss out on if we did not stutter. We talked about times our stutters had brought humor into our lives. 

I do not know if I ever convinced my students that stuttering was valuable and good. However, I do think I convinced them that it was fine to stutter and that they did not need to be fluent to be great communicators, nor was it their fault if others treated them poorly. They realized the harder they tried to be fluent speakers, the harder it was for them to speak. Once the burden of fluency was lifted off their shoulders, they could concentrate on stuttering with ease. Ironically, this often led to less frequent stuttering of shorter duration. More importantly, they stopped blaming themselves for stuttering. They no longer blamed themselves for bullying but blamed their aggressors. This did not make their stuttering go away. It did not guarantee they would be completely comfortable stuttering, and it did not eliminate all their struggle behaviors. My students often talked about how they still felt pressure to be fluent and how difficult this pressure was to resist. The difference was that they no longer took this pressure as truth but something to confront, resist, and overcome. 

Therapy prioritized subjective well-being (i.e., ease of speaking) over normalization (i.e., fluency). Overwhelmingly, my students were receiving messages from their peers and society that stuttering, and disability in general, was bad and shameful. If we had focused on achieving and maintaining fluency, we would have been complicit in confirming society’s prejudices that yes, stuttering is bad, fluency is good, and they should try their hardest not to stutter. Instead we did our best to embrace who they were and find value in their experiences, even when stuttered.
