# Title: Dialoguing with autism: Deconstruction of autism discourse in Chinese and reconstruction of narrative agency

## Abstract

Sociocultural dynamics of autism-related terminology and the interplay between language, identity, and epistemic justice in the Global South are veiled. Employing a mixed-methods approach, including literature review, surveys, and interviews—the study examines how Chinese-language users perceive and conceptualize Chinese autism-related terms like GuDuZheng (孤独症, GDZ, “GuDu” means “isolation/loneliness” and “Zheng” means their extended versions. Through community collaboration and reflexive thematic analysis, the study addresses how these terms reflect broader social constructs of autism and how autistic individuals engage with diagnostic labels. The findings reveal that “Terms Are Socially Constructed” and “The Concept of Autism in the Chinese Context Has Been Typified as ZiBi (Self-closeness).” Despite societal stigmatization, individuals navigate these challenges by actively engaging with the diagnoses to reinterpret their implications within both Eastern and Western understandings. Drawing from Chinese philosophy such as “The unity of heaven and man,” “The equality of all beings,” and “The harmony,” individuals reconstruct personal narratives that counter mainstream discourses, fostering internal coherence and harmonious interpersonal interactions. As one participant articulated, “Loneliness embodies holistic completeness: Like Zhuangzi’s useless tree thriving in the wilderness, Buddhism’s suchness without fabrication, or Stoic’s alignment with natural reason.” To achieve this completeness, participants advocated that “Everyone from the Community Should Work Together,” suggesting concrete action steps for dismantling structural ableism. This study offers new insights into the development of an indigenous neurodiversity paradigm that resonates with the Chinese sociolinguistic context. It facilitates a quadruple dialogue between Eastern and Western, between researchers and community, among neurodiverse individuals, and between autistic individuals and diagnostic labels. Ultimately, this process enables the reclamation of cultural and individual autonomy in the Global South, empowering autistic individuals to reclaim narrative agency and enhance their well-being.

## Introduction

As a socially constructed phenomenon, language not only reflects reality but also actively shapes it (Au, 1983; Brown & Lenneberg, 1954). In illness narratives, terminology serves as a vehicle for power practices and a battleground for identity politics (Botha et al., 2023; Sinclair, 1999; Wu, 2025). In Western contexts, discussions surrounding autism and its associated language have grown increasingly rich and vigorous. The neurodiversity movement originating in North America has infused discussions on autism discourse with a powerful impetus for justice and rationality (den Houting, 2019; Dwyer, 2022a, 2022b). Participatory research frameworks and community partnerships have revolutionized knowledge production and practical models in the medical field (Cornwall & Jewkes, 1995; den Houting et al., 2021; Fletcher-Watson et al., 2019). However, in the Global South, autistic individuals<sup>1</sup>, parents, autism researchers, and specialists remain under the influence of the Northern and Western countries (Meekosha & Soldatic, 2011; Nair et al., 2024). Meanwhile, local communities’ cultural interpretations of neurological differences are systematically inactive. Although terminology operates at a micro-level, it reflects rich epistemological and methodological tensions. Against this backdrop, research on the justice of terminology and paradigms for health-related knowledge and practice in non-English-using Global South countries remains notably insufficient.

Autism, originally a Western term later adopted as a diagnostic category in medicine, encompasses diverse traits across social communication, behaviors, and (American Psychological Association (APA), 2013; World Health Organization (WHO), 2023). The perception and usage of this term vary across languages and contexts. In Chinese, the generation, introduction, and usage of autism-related terminology carry unique dynamics. On one hand, the definition and conceptual description of autism are entirely derived from Western frameworks (Mayor-Gross et al., 1963). However, upon its introduction to China, the term was almost simultaneously translated into two distinct Chinese terms: GuDuZheng (GDZ, 孤独症，GuDu means isolation/loneliness) and ZiBiZheng (ZBZ, 自闭症, ZiBi means self-closeness/closing-off). These terms differ in their literal meanings, pronunciations, and social metaphors, opening room for comparison and deliberation. On the other hand, the Chinese translation of autism is medicalized through the suffix Zheng (症, which means disease/symptoms), endowed with the pathological and deficient nature of the terms. How individuals navigate this dual social construction—marked by relative ambiguity (bi-translation) and certainty (pathologization) in Chinese contexts—is integral to autistic individuals’ selfconstruction of narratives agency and their well-being (Hughner & Kleine, 2004).

Adopting a social constructivist and critical lens (Berger & Luckmann, 2016; Phillips, 2023) to deconstruct the generation, dissemination, and public perception of autism-related terminology in Chinese, we interrogate how individuals engage with medical labels to deconstruct their meanings and reconfigure cognition, foregrounding power-asymmetric epistemic authority while reclaiming moral agency through counternarratives—thereby exposing interwoven epistemic injustices and ethical dilemmas (Bhakuni & Abimbola, 2021; Fricker, 2007). We focus on two research questions (RQ):

RQ1: How is the term Autism constructed in the Chinese context, what concepts does it reflect, and how is it used?

RQ2: Through what dialogic processes do individuals interact with diagnostic labels to cognitively reconstruct self-narrative?

To understand how Chinese-language users<sup>2</sup> conceptualize and shape the local dynamics of autism-related terminology, we first contrast Sino-Western discursive divergences. By collecting usage frequencies of autism-related terminology across Chinese publications, databases, social media platforms, and major search engines trend indices, and reviewing debates over the translation and selection of autism terminology within Chinese contexts, we map the characteristics and current landscape of autism-related terminology usage in Chinese contexts. Through an online survey and in-depth interviews investigating terminology preferences among Chinese-language users from diverse backgrounds, coupled with reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) of interview content (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2019), we collaboratively trace how their lexical choices emerge through situated negotiations of identity, lived experiences, and conceptual understandings of autism, while critically engaging with the power dynamics that constrain or enable particular terminological practices. Methodologically, we adopted a community-partnership model, collaborating with autistic communities across all research phases—from design to dissemination. Initially framed as a matter of divergent terminology and conceptual understandings of autism (RQ1), we have been continually inspired by the depth of community feedback and discussions, discovering the dynamic agency, enthusiasm, and critical subjectivity emerging in these debates<sup>3</sup>. We also observed how subjectivity progressively reclaimed discursive agency within these negotiations (RQ2).

Consequently, this study positions itself as a reflexive critique for autism researchers and neurodiversity advocates in the Global South, addressing both the paucity of localized conceptual debates and providing pathways for indigenizing neurodiversity movements. By illuminating the multilayered tensions underlying terminological divergences and contradictions, we offered evidence for developing destigmatized and culturally-nuanced terminology policies. Practically, we propose guidelines for clinicians to conduct neuro-affirmative diagnostic dialogues, emphasizing authentic <sup>4</sup> engagement. Furthermore, we advocate communitycollaborative frameworks in global health research to dismantle epistemic hierarchies and foster pluriversal knowledge systems.

## More than terminology: A critical examination of autism discourse

The ongoing international academic discussion around the formal terminology and related language of autism goes beyond term choices. It reflects deeper ontological debates about what autism is and how autism should be defined. Initially, Bleuler combined autos (meaning “self” or “automatic”) and -ism (meaning “state/condition”) to describe a state of mental detachment from reality in individuals with schizophrenia characteristics observed in children, such as social isolation, and preference for fixed routines, marking a distinction between autism and the concept of schizophrenia (Kanner, 1943). With the advancement of neuroscience, the concept of autism gradually became associated with terms such as neurological (Damasio & Maurer, 1978), development (Rutter, 1978), and spectrum (Wing, 1997), which reflected changes in diagnostic criteria and nomenclature (APA & Zhang, 2015). The history of the term autism demonstrates how medical authorities pathologized human mental and behavioral phenomena, categorizing them as deficits, abnormalities, or dysfunctions, and integrating them into the medical system.

In recent years, the terminology related to autism continues to evolve. In Englishusing countries, debates between “person-first language” (PFL, such as “individual with autism”) and “identity-first language” (IFL, such as “autistic individual”) reflect a fundamental disagreement over the essence of autism: should it be seen as an external pathological affliction or as a core component of an individual’s identity (Botha et al., 2023; Buijsman et al., 2023; Vivanti, 2020)? Empirical studies show that autistic communities in English-using countries or regions prefer IFL significantly more than non-autistic groups (e.g., Botha et al., 2023; Bury et al., 2023; Keating et al., 2023). This choice of language practice has become a form of asserting individuality, challenging the professional choices suggested by academic associations (APA, 1992). autism. Disputes over terminology often intertwine with mor enta cognitive frameworks. The active participation of the represents a critical turning point—through first-person narratives and critical theories led by autism scholars (den Houting, 2019; Dinishak & Akhtar, 2023; Sinclair, 1999; Stenning, 2024; M. Yergeau, 2013; M. Yergeau, 2018), the traditional “deficit paradigm” is being reconstructed. Simultaneously, research paradigms are evolving. Participatory methods (e.g., Community Based Participatory Research, Participatory Action Research) are challenging the one-way power structures of traditional medical research (Armitt et al., 2024; Batalden et al., 2016; Cornish et al., 2023; Cornwall & Jewkes, 1995; Fletcher-Watson et al., 2019), repositioning autistic individuals as “experiential experts” (Milton, 2014). This paradigm shift not only requires researchers to design research questions in collaboration with community members but also challenges the presupposition of a pathological and cure-oriented perspective. When autistic individuals transition from being “research subjects” to “research collaborators” and even “researchers,” the legitimacy of knowledge authority shifts from professional qualifications to lived experiences, thereby addressing epistemic injustice in the knowledge production of autism.

The neurodiversity movement that emerged in North America in the 1990s marks a formal turn in autism discourse and research paradigms (Blume, 1998). The goal is not simply to reconstruct the conceptual boundaries or reinterpret the meaning of autism, but to systematically deconstruct the binary framework of “pathological” and “nonpathological,” based on the scientific fact of neurological diversity (den Houting, 2019, 2019; Dwyer, 2022b). This movement fundamentally critiques the ableism inherent in traditional medical models (Botha et al., 2023; Bottem eutel et al., 2021; Geelhand et al., 2023; Kapp et al., 2013). By emphasizing the naturalness and legitimacy of neurodevelopmental differences, the n urodiversity movement provides a new ontological foundation for autism and other neurodevelopmental variations. This movement has given rise to new terms such as “neurotypical” and “neurodivergent” (Walker, 2014), and encompasses advocacy, criticism, lobbying, and the influence on policy-making and scientific research design and methodology, e.g., neurodiversity paradigms and approaches. Currently, this is most prominently reflected in the ongoing discussions regarding the use of autism-related language in academic research and how to frame the research question and methodologies, including the promotion of nonableist terminology, the affirmation of neurodiversity, and the support for communitybased co-creation or participatory research frameworks. In this process, the neurodiversity movement goes beyond terminology reform to become a powerful force in reconstructing the agency of disability communities.

## The multiplicity of autism terminology in the Chinese context

The introduction and conceptual construction of autism in the Chinese context reveal significant multiplicity. First, there exists bi-translation process, where autism is translated as both “孤独症” (Gūdú zhèng) and “自闭症” (Zìbì zhèng). Secondly, the medicalization of the translation is evident through the use of the character “症” (meaning “disease” or “condition”). Thirdly, the special grammatical structure of the Chinese language—its tendency for nominalization and the ambiguity of possessive markers (Hsu, 2019; Lao et al., 2024; Zhu, 1993) dissolves the tension between IFL and PFL. This unique linguistic context has shaped the conceptual construction of autism in the Chinese-using world, significantly influencing the social understanding of autism in China.

The bi-translation introduces distinct cultural connotations into the localization of the autism concept. “孤独” (GuDu, loneliness/solitude) represents a human affective expresses an emotional, social, and relational state, while “自闭” (closing-off oneself) conveys a more self-directed action. There is no consensus among Chinese scholars on which translation is more “accurate,” “rigorous,” or “civilized.” The term GDZ originated from the Chinese translation of Mayer-Gross’s Clinical Psychiatry in 1963 (Mayor-Gross et al., 1963), and became institutionalized through the first clinical report in China (Tao, 1982). In contrast, ZBZ (自闭症) derives from the Japanese “自閉症” (Washimi, E, 1952), and was formally reported in academic publications in 1966 (Lai, 2019). This duality extended to related long formal terms like the autism spectrum disorder, with both “孤独症谱系障碍 (GDZ spectrum disorder)” (WHO, 2023a; WHO TEAM Mental Health (MHE), 2022) and “自闭症谱系障碍 (ZBZ spectrum disorder)” (Cao & Fang, 2008) appearing contemporaneously, further complicating the standardization process of the terminology. The divergent usage of different Chinese autism-related terms in academic and official texts reflects the inherent conceptual inconsistencies. Publications from the WHO have alternated between GDZ and ZBZ (WHO, 2023b; WHO TEAM Mental Health (MHE), 2022), while the United Nations (UN) system predominantly uses ZBZ (UN, 2007). The Chinese Classification of Mental Disorders, 3rd Edition (2001) institutionalized GDZ into diagnostic standards, yet the DSM-5 Chinese version (APA & Zhang, 2015) syntactically juxtaposes both terms under the category. Despite the international acceptance of autism spectrum disorder as a standard term, the China Disabled Persons’ Federation (CDPF) continued to advocate for the shorter form, GDZ, as of 2022 (CDPF, 2022). This indicates a preference for concise terminology in the Chinese context, which contrasts with the longer terms used internationally.

Further analysis reveals that attempts to connect the term autism with other related terms to construct more precise concepts have not been entirely effective in the Chinese context. Unlike English, the Chinese terms of autism lack adjectival forms, forcing users to rely on nominalized structures (e.g., “孤独症儿童,” meaning “autistic child”). This nominalization, combined with frequent omissions of possessive markers (“的”, De [’s/adjective form]) and the copula verb (“是,” Shì [be]) (Hsu, 2019; Zhu, 1993), blurs the distinction between IFL and PFL. For instance, “孤独症儿童” could be interpreted as “a child with autism” (PFL) or as “an autistic child” (IFL), despite some scholars arguing that “孤独症儿童” in Chinese has no possessive structure, only appositive structure (see also Supplementary Table S1 & S2). These linguistic features effectively obscure the clear attribution of subjectivity.

## Epistemic repair and localization challenges

Global health research has long been dominated by Northern perspectives. The discussion of terminology reflects the phenomenon of epistemic injustice (Wang & Ye, 2023) prevalent in Global South countries—where diagnostic standards and naming systems are heavily dependent on Western medical discourse, often disregarding local cultural interpretations. As Chinese researchers and practitioners, we acknowledge that much of our work follows Northern perspectives, and transferring “advanced” theories and methods to China is our responsibility. However, we do not believe that this transfer is entirely reasonable or just. In this study, our theoretical framework is based on social constructionism, and our position is to serve as communicators between different cultures and bodies of knowledge, spanning East and West, North and South, as well as between researchers and communities. Cao and Zhu come from psychological backgrounds and have considerable experiences in deep communication and learning with scholars of Western analytic psychologists. Wang has a background in human geography and extensive experience in qualitative research.

For example, the concept of neurodiversity was first introduced in 2016 in Chinese, and there are only a handful of academic discussions on it (Jing, 2024; Lao & Zhu, 2024; Pilei & Qingshan, 2024; Zeng & Yu, 2016). Participatory research models are also relatively new in both autistic research (Angell et al., 2023; Armitt et al., 2024; Pilei & Qingshan, 2024) and the non-English world. Moreover, these and it was only in 2024 that the first paper authored by published in a Chinese academic journal (Pilei & Qingsh Chinese scholars have provided meaningful reflections on these issu proposing that autism research within the cultural context should reveal structural inequalities along a “neurodevelopmental” fault line and explore how local cultural resources might offer more possibilities for accommodating this unique mode of existence (An, 2021). They have also advocated for a more pluralistic perspective on disability in China (Zhang & Pan, 2024), emphasizing the need to critically examine cultural conceptions of disability (Niu, 2021) and challenging the neurological reductionist inherent in neurodiversity and ignoring the importance of the body in meaning construction (Wang & Ye, 2023). However, these reflections often stop short of addressing methodological and practical implications. There remains a significant gap in translating these theoretical critiques into actionable research and clinical practices that produce knowledge valuable to the autistic community.

In the Chinese context, autism research still faces challenges in terms of reflecting on Northern theories, achieving sufficient localization, bridging the gap between academia and community, and increasing awareness and understanding of neurodiversity concepts and community-based collaboration methods. Therefore, Chinese autism researchers urgently need to reflect on the current epistemic injustice in autism, find a balance between Northern theories and local cultural practices in terminology discussions, and break down the systemic barriers between academia, medicine, and the community. By adopting a community collaboration framework, the current study can foster more inclusive and culturally sensitive language discussions, which will contribute to advancing research and practice, and influence the generation and dissemination of autism knowledge.

## Theoretical framework

This study aims to explore the perspectives of autistic individuals within contexts of epistemic injustice and marginalization while proposing a more inclusive and culturally sensitive research framework informed by traditional Chinese modes of thinking.

This study synthesizes multiple theoretical perspectives and frameworks— spanning epistemic injustice theory, counterstory theory, neurodiversity approaches, and Chinese philosophical traditions—to critically examine autism discourse within the sociocultural contexts of China. The integrated framework operates through distinct yet complementary mechanisms: 1) epistemic injustice theory diagnoses systemic marginalization patterns; 2) counterstory theory illuminates agential narrative reclamation; 3) neurodiversity approaches reposit autism as neurological variations; 4) Chinese philosophy provides culturally-grounded tools for enhancing personal wellbeing and maintaining social harmony. Collectively, these perspectives constitute an analytical matrix for deconstructing and reconstituting autism terminology’s sociolinguistic meanings.

## Epistemic injustice in autism discourse

Epistemic injustice denotes the devaluation of marginalized groups’ knowledge claims under structural power asymmetries (Bhakuni & Abimbola, 2021; Fricker, 2007). Within autism research, this materializes through the systemic exclusion of autistic individuals from epistemic production, wherein mainstream narratives privilege professional perspectives while discounting autistic lived experience (Catala et al., 2021; Chapman & Carel, 2022). Neuronormative biases further undermine the credibility of autistic testimony, engendering epistemic disablement via environmental barriers to cognitive participation. Such dynamics precipitate testimonial and hermeneutical injustices, necessitating epistemic enablement strategies to restore equitable knowledge ecosystems. Aligned with this critique, our study explicitly confronts historical power imbalances between researchers and autistic communities in China, implementing participatory empowerment across research phases to counteract epistemic marginalization.

## Counterstory praxis

Narrative resistance through counterstories enables marginalized groups to reclaim moral agency and repair collective identity by subverting oppressive master narratives (Nelson, 2001). Autistic counter-narratives operate through three interconnected dimensions:

1. Explanatory force: Counterstories, which utilize first-person narratives to explain one’s experiences to oneself and others, can reveal how dominant (or “master”) narratives impose limitations on the social roles of autistic individuals by promoting exclusionary ideologies;

2. Correlation to action: The alternative narratives such as using neurodiversity approaches to reconfigure self-conception should be strongly correlated with the autistic person’s action; and

3. Heft: Identity-constituting narratives are built around those aspects of autistic individuals’ lives that hold the greatest personal significance — aspects that often sharply contrast with what master narratives define as important. As a result, counterstories have the potential to enhance societal understanding of autism, foster social change, and reduce stigma by prompting shifts in public attitudes and policy frameworks. (Stenning, 2024).

This tripartite model informs our analysis of how Chinese autistic individuals renegotiate diagnostic labels through meaning-making practices.

## Neurodiversity from a cross-cultural perspective

Positioning autism as a natural expression of human diversity, neurodiversity paradigms contest pathologization while acknowledging autism’s composite status as disability, difference, and identity (Walker, 2014). However, conceptual indeterminacy persists, with interpretations ranging from societal accommodation to individual trait emphases (Dwyer, 2022b). Neurodiversity approaches in the Global North have also been criticized for neglecting their practical relationships with socio-political contexts and for insufficiently addressing institutional ableism (Jones & Orchard, 2024). This underscores the need to contextualize neurodiversity within local cultural and philosophical frameworks. For instance, Chinese culture offers culturally resonant resources for reconceptualizing autism through its emphasis on harmony, balance, and interconnectedness.

## Cultural perspectives on autism: Holism and high-context challenges

## The contrast between holism and dualism

Traditional Chinese philosophy advocates the cosmological principle of interdependent unity between humanity and nature, which eschews dualistic categorizations. This contrasts starkly with Western biomedical paradigms embodied in DSM-5 diagnostics, which reduce complex human experiences into quantifiable symptom clusters (DSM-5, 2013). The epistemic hegemony of the Global North knowledge systems has perpetuated developing nations’ reliance on externally imposed frameworks (Alatas, 1976) — a phenomenon starkly evident in China’s uncritical adoption of Western autism evaluation models. Such conceptual colonialism (“Captive Mind,” Alatas, 1976) not only contradicts China’s holistic philosophical traditions but systematically marginalizes autistic individuals through diagnostic reductionism. Consequently, Chinese autistic self-advocates ground their counter-narratives in indigenous cultural resources to resist Western epistemic dominance.

## High-context communication barriers

Chinese sociolinguistic norms prioritize relational harmony (He, 和) through high-context communication—where meaning is embedded in nonverbal cues and shared cultural knowledge (Hall, 1976; J. Jiang et al., 2025; Li, 2011). For autistic individuals, this may pose multilayered challenges:

1. Decoding implicit social expectations amid neuronormative demands;

2. Navigating pressures to perform “altruistic conformity”—often sacrificing selfadvocacy for collective harmony; and,

3. Countering hermeneutical injustice when communication differences are misattributed to moral deficits.

In a nutshell, considering these linguistic and cultural characteristics, our community-engaged approach centers on autistic-led communication modalities and reaffirms iteratively throughout the research that the research is conducted with and centered on autistic individuals. This praxis aims to subvert epistemic injustice by repositioning autistic individuals as epistemic authorities in knowledge production.

Grounded in a social constructivist framework with a critical approach (Phillips, 2023), we employed RTA (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021) to analyze participants’ views on preferred and offensive terms. RTA offers significant flexibility in both theoretical and analytical scope. Importantly, our focus was not on the factual correctness of opinions but on understanding how participants’ narratives engage with the issues at hand (Berger & Luckmann, 2016), ultimately generating key themes through data immersion.

1. Familiarization with Data: All authors read and discussed the first 100 freetext responses to become familiar with the content and context.

2. Inductive Coding: Two authors (LU, ZH) initially coded the preliminary responses, generating provisional codes that were refined as their understanding evolved. Subsequently, three authors (LU, LF, DJ) independently coded the complete dataset, resolving discrepancies through whole team discussions at 25%, 50%, and 100% of the process. The coding process was iterative, with regular refinements to capture the complexity and nuances of the data. In terms of approach, while our primary focus was on identifying the latent meanings behind the data—uncovering hidden assumptions and interpretations—we also recognized that the semantic level of the data contributed valuable insights. Consequently, both semantic and latent coding methods were applied.

3. Generating Initial Themes: The authors held several meetings to review the coded data and identify meanings and relationships among initial codes, thereby generating candidate themes and sub-themes.

4. Reviewing Themes: Coherent patterns were identified by examining the coded data and exploring interconnections within it.

5. Defining, Re-defining, and Naming Themes: Themes were refined and named, incorporating feedback from autism community members to ensure they authentically reflected the community’s multiplicity of lived experiences and needs.

6. Writing: Drawing on these insights and community feedback, a research report was composed detailing the significance of each theme and its implications for enhancing the experiences of autistic individuals. That report was then submitted to the Journal for review.

Phase two deepening analysis: Terminology interaction and community feedback

After receiving the reviewers’ feedback, and based on their suggestions, we proceeded with the second phase of the study. In phase two, we interviewed 11 autistic individuals from diverse backgrounds, two experienced autism reporters, and one media representative from an autism-related non-government organization (NGO) (Table 1). These interviews deepened our understanding of terminology perception and construction. Using RTA with community collaboration, we finalized our thematic framework and developed a practical thematic map.

Community Collaboration: Building on phase one, we invited online autistic community members to evaluate preliminary findings<sup>5</sup> (Nicolaidis et al., 2019; Pilei & Qingshan, 2024). We also held one-on-one online interviews via WeChat (text/voice) or Tencent Meeting (video). After each interview, participants reviewed their transcripts and provided feedback on the thematic map, ensuring that emerging themes reflected their lived experiences.

Emergence of New Themes: Feedback from phase two generated two new themes—“Terminology is socially constructed” and “Everyone from the community should work together”—which were integrated into a cohesive framework.

## Results

## Divergence between official, public, and academic terminology use

Data indicate that while GDZ is the official term, ZBZ consistently dominates public discourse, with its popularity on social media and search engines steadily rising. Following the endorsement of GDZ by the CDPF in 2022 (CDPF, 2022), the use of GDZ in mainstream media increased, yet ZBZ remained prevalent on social platforms.

In academia, usage of both terms was balanced, although longer forms like GDZ Spectrum Disorder, were preferred, despite rarely appearing in public contexts (Supplementary Tables S3 & S4, Supplementary Fig. S1–S3).

Critical review (Supplementary Table S5) further shows that Chinese academic discussions on autism translations predominantly focus on Kanner’s or Bleuler’s definitions and semantics, neglecting the lived experiences of autistic individuals. This medicalized framing of autism constrains autistic agencies, obscuring potentially negative social connotations.

## Preference and offensiveness rankings for formal terminologies

After excluding 73 of the 1,089 responses, we analyzed 1,016 valid surveys (Table 2 & Supplementary Table S6). Results indicate that over half of the participants favored GDZ Spectrum Disorder as the least offensive and most acceptable term, while nearly two-thirds deemed ZBZ the most offensive (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S7-S11). In the Chinese context, the autism translations GDZ and ZBZ exemplify the socially constructed nature of terminology. GuDu (loneliness) or ZiBi (self-closeness) does not fully capture the auto in autism even when compared literally. While Western discourse views autism as a cognitive pattern (e.g., self-oriented thinking) (Evans, 2017), these Chinese terms emphasize a state, action, or perception. In essence, the concept of autism in the West is seen as an abstract, consistent trait, whereas GuDu or ZiBi requires contextual interpretation. As DISH WASHER noted,

“[P]eople with different ways of thinking have their interpretations of the

same words and things.”

Moreover, using Zheng (disease/symptoms) frames autism as a disease, reinforcing stigma. Our data reveal wide variation in interpreting GuDu (loneliness) and ZiBi (self-closeness):

⚫ Loneliness is seen as a subjective emotion (“the feeling of being unaccompanied”

[CABBAGE]) or as a self-contained state (“unrelated to the outside world” [no.6, specialist]).

⚫ Self-closeness is understood as an active process (“actively closing oneself” [no.321, autistic]) or as a state of immobilization (“unable to move physically, verbally, or mentally” [DISH WASHER]).

This blend of interpretative uncertainty (from dual translations and varied meanings) and certainty (from the medicalized use of Zheng) defines the social construction of autism terminology in Chinese. Additionally, the high-context nature of Chinese means that fixed terms alone cannot fully convey the concept of autism (Hall, 1976). As PIPPI remarked, “I tend to respond more based on context and actual situations.”

In contrast, Western (Indo-European) languages generally offer a single, unambiguous translation of autism, focusing discussions on its conceptual meaning rather than its literal form (Saussure, 1916/1980). However, in Chinese—where each character can be analyzed independently, and meaning is highly context-dependent— users engage in more diverse interpretations when constructing and deconstructing terminology.

## Theme 2: The concept of autism in the Chinese context has been typified as ZiBi (self-closeness)

This theme examines how the concept of autism, after widespread social dissemination in the Chinese context, has been reduced to the stereotype of actively closing off oneself and the implications of this simplification. This reduction follows two paths: one path is to disregard the social challenges autistic individuals face, shifting blame onto them; and the other is to exaggerate the condition as severe, incurable, or even dehumanizing.

While autistic individuals may not inherently resent self-closeness, its evolution and dissemination have raised issues. As CHY mentioned, “I originally had no particular feelings about the terms self-closeness or loneliness they were just adjectives like ‘red,’ ‘hard,’ or ‘sweet.’” Similarly, SALTED FISH observed, “Doctors likely did not intend to stigmatize when naming the condition; the problem lies in how the term has been distorted over time.”

However, what autistic individuals oppose is the current neuronormative society depriving them of the right to define autism on their own terms, enforcing a singular stereotype that erases individual differences. On one hand, autistic individuals criticize the social injustice behind this mainstream singular narrative; on the other hand, they challenge this narrative by reinterpreting self-closeness and asserting their agency (Lucas, 2016).

Sub-theme 1: Autistic people aren’t actively closing off themselves (no.182, autistic)

Participants rejected the oversimplified view that autistic individuals actively isolate themselves. For example, BULL, from a third-tier Chinese city, explained that in his community, ZiBi implies a child is always living in their own world—suggesting intellectual disability and poor communication skills.

While some appreciate the focus on the self, they argue that closure is neither an active nor passive process. ASDOG stated:

“The root of ‘autism’ is auto, meaning ‘self,’ so using ‘self’ is appropriate...[B]ut closure is...neither active nor passive.”

Respondents attributed social difficulties and burnout to sensory overload and processing complex information simultaneously. SALTED FISH explained that autistic individuals may need to “immerse themselves in their thoughts,” while ASDog noted they “seek answers within themselves.” One parent stated:

“[Autistic people] are absolutely not closing off themselves. In contrast, what

they may exhibit from birth is a natural tendency to be more open, direct,

trusting, unguarded, and undisguised to reveal their true selves...The term

[self-closeness] is misleading as it suggests a psychological state in which

people shut themselves off due to emotional experiences like sadness that can

arise from trauma” (no.825).

Another parent added: “Compared to ‘self-closure,’ ‘loneliness’ better reflects reality: Autistic individuals do not actively shut themselves off but struggle to find appropriate ways to connect with others.” (no.182)

This misunderstanding partly stems from the negative connotations of selfcloseness, which suggests isolation due to sadness or trauma, while ignoring its potential as a positive, reflective state. As SALTED FISH remarked: “Self-closeness is a crucial part of my life...to process and reflect on the information from the world.”

In contrast, neurotypicals often assume that someone described as self-closed lacks agency and subjectivity, which fail to recognize the double empathy problem (Milton et al., 2022; Milton, 2012)—a view captured by the Ancient Chinese saying, “You are not a fish; how can you know the joy of the fish?” (Zhuangzi, c350–250 BCE/1891) This perspective is reflected in journalist FOREST’s observation that autistic people were “like little animals... staring blankly in the sun,” and their lives “seem lost, yet they don’t feel bored, and they don’t have to consider anything… They just naively muddle through life.”

DISH WASHER explained that “differences in behavioral logics and sensory sensitivities” likely account for the divergent interpretations of self-closeness between autistic and neurotypical individuals. Many autistic respondents recalled past interactions where, on one hand, they “didn’t understand neurotypical social rules (WISDOM),” and on the other hand, they found that neurotypical individuals “often seem incomprehensible to what [autistic people] try to express” (BULL).

Some respondents mentioned that following neurotypical-led social interactions was overwhelming. As CHY noted:

“For a ‘well [neurotypically] socialized’ autistic, I’m accommodating and adapting to neurotypical [norms and expectations] most of the time. Neurotypicals might not notice my fatigue or emotions… exhaustion... the cognitive load of adhering to neurotypical social rules.”

Autistic individuals ultimately hope society will come to understand that, much like how few truly believe “few truly believe that wife cakes contain wives” (SALTED-FISH), they too should not be confined to a reductive stereotype of being self-closed.

Sub-theme 2: Zibi(self-closeness) downplays the plights faced by neurodivergent people (no.714, autistic)

The sarcastic internet meme “I’m self-closed” (Fig. 4) trivializes the genuine challenges faced by autistic individuals. For many, self-closeness is seen as a temporary, negative state that one can easily overcome (DISH WASHER). JADE noted that people often view autism as “an excuse for avoiding learning and thinking.”

In contrast, for autistic individuals, self-closeness is a profound, enduring state affecting both mind and body, not something one can simply “snap out of (DISH WASHER).” As, participant no. 321, an autistic individual, explained:

“[Since] punchlines like ‘I’m self-closed’ have become clichés, they further reinforce stereotypes about ASD, [leading others] to ignore that it’s a disorder needing support.”

Here, we observe an example of epistemic injustice (Fricker, 2007). When autistic individuals with “fewer” support needs label themselves as ZBZ, they often encounter dismissive remarks such as, “Be more open,” or “You’re not closed-off enough.” This reflects a misunderstanding of their condition, treating it as something minor or easily overcome, instead of recognizing it as a valid and persistent challenge. The experience of being told to “try harder” or “change yourself” invalidates their knowledge of their condition, reducing their lived experience to a stereotype. These stereotypical responses contribute to epistemic injustice by denying these individuals the opportunity to have their voices heard and their needs acknowledged. Over time, this leads to a diminished willingness to share their struggles, further marginalizing their experiences and even

causing them to question their identity.

Autistic participant (no. 714) recounted others’ absurd reactions when she disclosed her autistic identity using the term ZBZ (self-closeness):

“I don’t think you’re self-closed. Ah, you’re overthinking it. If you’re selfclosed, just go outside, get more sunshine, and change yourself.”

Such responses not only invalidate their experiences but also discourage autistic individuals from expressing their true challenges, sometimes even making them question their own identity.

Sub-theme 3: ZBZ is widely used and has become inseparable from stereotypes (no.627, autistic)

Participants observed that ZBZ has become heavily laden with negative stereotypes and connotations of extreme severity. One parent remarked, “In the culture of our country, ZBZ and self-closeness carry derogatory connotations, ambiguities, and deep-rooted misunderstandings” (no.67). A non-autistic individual noted that “selfcloseness is as common a household term as cancer, and adults advise their children to avoid autistic children....” (no.356)

SALTED FISH recounted an online discussion about autism, reflecting how the public has reduced autistic individuals into a ZiBi monolithic representation. Below is an excerpt from that online discussion:

“If someone communicates online, how can they be considered ZiBi? Only

those who don’t want to communicate are truly ZiBi.”

She compared this to the absurd notion that “wife cakes contain wives.”

ZBZ is now associated with terms like stupid, extremely severe, terrifying, dangerous, hopeless, lacking empathy, and unemotional. Such labels lead to harsh judgments of autistic individuals, as reflected in news headlines provided by FLOWER like “ZBZ families face resistance in public housing” and “Our neighborhood now has 17 ‘mentally ill’ residents.”

CHY explained that the strong association between the term ZBZ and mental disorders leads many to conflate autism with psychiatric or mood disorders, unfairly labeling autistic individuals as troublesome or violent. Some media outlets even highlight instances of self-harm or aggression among autistic children to attract attention, further cementing these stereotypes (e.g., Chen, 2025).

In the nuances of our interview with JARVIS, a media representative from a wellknown autism NGO in China, we can see how their biased reporting on the autistic community further damages an already impaired image of autistic individuals: “[As media workers], we always consider how to attract more audience attention. I think there’s not much we can do about this (reducing biased reporting). Reporting inevitably leads to distortion. What we can do is encourage the audience to remain vigilant...”

## Theme 3: Autism is a human diverse phenomenon

Confronting such ZiBi unilateral narratives, autistic individuals need to make sense of their past and current experiences. To repair their damaged identities and reclaim their agencies, they can develop narratives that are coherent and sufficiently broad in scope (Nelson, 2001). As Stenning highlights in her work (Stenning, 2024, p.

X), “For people who are devalued according to their assumed social status, actions may be constrained by environments designed for ‘normal’ people, but our understanding may not be.” This insight reflects how autistic individuals can actively reshape the narrative around autism, seeking empowerment and self-definition in a world that often marginalizes them.

Thus, this theme reflects participants’ recognition of autism as a manifestation of human diversity—emphasizing individuality, agency, subjectivity, and autonomy— while challenging oppressive, stereotypical master narratives prevalent in Chinese autism discourse. They advocate for an inclusive framework centered on individual experiences, rejecting simplified views. While sub-themes focused on preferences for the term GDZ Spectrum Disorder—rooted in the notion that “Loneliness Is The Destiny of Mankind” (sub-theme 1)—the broader theme encapsulates participants’ desire to discourse. They advocate for a new, inclusive framework that centers individual experiences to develop counter-narratives (Nelson, 2001). Given that the term GDZ is used less frequently than ZBZ (Supplementary Table S1 & S2)—and therefore carries fewer societal metaphors—it holds greater potential for narrative reclamation. As CHY explained:

“GDZ is perceived as less stereotypical than ZBZ not because the term ‘loneliness’ inherently mitigates stigma, but because its negative associations are not as deeply entrenched as those of ‘self-closeness’ at present.”

Under this theme, participants express a dual reality: “We’re the same”

(highlighting universal human experiences and human rights, sub-theme 1: Loneliness is the destiny of humankind) and “We’re different” (acknowledging unique individual traits and challenges, sub-theme 2: Spectrum represents thousands of people and thousands of faces). They argue that autism transcends a mere disease; it encompasses both difference and disability (sub-theme 3: Don’t treat autism as a disease that must be cured). As PIPPI insightfully captured,

“I believe we must first acknowledge that disabled and non-disabled individuals differ in resources and challenges, yet we share a common humanity with equal rights to access what suits and supports us.”

Sub-theme 1: Gudu (loneliness) is the destiny of humankind (no.150, autistic)

Participants view loneliness as a universal, inevitable experience that affirms being rather than a pathological defect. A specialist remarked, “Born to be human, loneliness is inescapable... [GDZ] enables empathy.” (no.662) Chinese thought, with its emphasis on dialectical reasoning and practical experience (Chai et al., 2024), regards loneliness as a dynamic balance between inner harmony and external expectations. This mode of thinking is referred to as historical reason, which stands in stark contrast to Western logical reason. The latter posits that truth exists beyond sensory experience and relies solely on speculative and analytical methods (Liu, 2020). In other words, when understanding autistic individuals’ circumstances, Chinese-language users tend to rely more on their own practical experiences. Therefore, participants argued that framing autism through universal loneliness—a relatable experience—could enhance public resonance and support. As one parent stated: “This name conveys a sense of needing warmth and proactive assistance from others, leaving a favorable impression. (no.929)”

Several autistic participants found that GDZ accurately describes their state: “I’m lonely and lack friends” (no.711), and “We may be different, but we should not be excluded... [We] hope not to be so lonely.” (no.634).

In Chinese philosophical traditions, loneliness transcends mere isolation; it signifies a dynamic equilibrium between one’s spiritual aspirations and self-contained wholeness, rooted in traditions that valorize inner harmony over external validation (Chian, 2009). As Philosopher Zhuangzi described, “He chiefly cared to occupy himself with the spirit-like operation of heaven and earth, and did not try to rise above the myriads of things. He did not condemn the agreements and differences of others, so that he might live in peace with the prevalent views.” (Zhuangzi, 350–250 BCE/1891) Participant CHARLIE also elaborated:

“The classical view of loneliness embodies holistic completeness—like Zhuangzi’s useless tree thriving in the wilderness, Buddhism’s suchness without fabrication, or Stoic’s alignment with natural reason.”

Sub-theme 2: Spectrum Represents Thousands of People and Thousands of Faces (no.911, parent)

Participants emphasized that the term spectrum encompasses the broadest range of experiences, acknowledging vastly divergent traits without derogatory implications. Participants claimed that “spectrum covers the widest range” (no.769, autistic) and that “different individuals can have very different characteristics” (no.276, non-autistic).

This diversity extends beyond behavioral patterns to encompass individual’s needs, strengths, interests, and developmental trajectories. A parent explained that the spectrum reveals autism as “not a simply black-and-white affair” (no.900). WISDOM, an autistic parent and elementary school teacher, observed,

“I’ve never seen two similar [autistic] children. Each has a unique profile.

While they share core traits... their manifestations differ entirely.”

For autistic individuals, the term spectrum fosters “self-awareness, awareness of others like them, and awareness of other people.” (CABBAGE) Many remarked that self-closeness (ZiBi) fails to capture the diverse experiences on the spectrum; one autistic participant stated, “Self-closeness is just one of the many manifestations on the spectrum (no.744)”, and another stated, “This saying is not comprehensive in a narrow sense. (no.650)”

Sub-theme 3: Don’t treat autism as a disease that must be cured (no.764, autistic)

Autistic participants and parents advocated for a more precise and destigmatized understanding of autism—not as a disease to be cured but as a condition requiring individualized support. While some participants acknowledged autism as a medical condition, their critique focused on how autism is moralized as evil (e.g., “evil must be expelled through treatment” [PIPPI]), thus resulting in self-blame—“having this disease is my fault” (PIPPI)—rather than fostering collective efforts to improve autistic lives.

This sub-theme examines the preference for full-length terms despite their limited use beyond academic/medical contexts. Participants framed this preference through the lens of Zheng (症, disease – pathologizing focus) and Zhang’ai (障碍, disorder – systemic barriers), noting that the Zhang’ai “mitigates the feeling of illness as opposed to ‘Zheng’ (no.725, parent)”. Participants noted that full-length terms better reflect autism as a condition “involving both the individual and society,” (CABBAGE), and “with additional qualifiers like ‘spectrum’ help dispel misconceptions.” (CHY) However, some acknowledged the practical limits for the dissemination of longer terminologies, as FOREST, a journalist, admitted:

“[S]horter terms like GDZ are easier to communicate. Even I struggled to

articulate the full-length term just now.”

Despite linguistic challenges, autistic individuals emphasized that autism is not a curable disease but a natural neurodiverse manifestation. They asserted their agency and individuality, as CHARLIE noted:

“Autistic individuals express themselves differently in terms of both inclination still possess unique personalities and perspectives, which make them autonomous subjects just like anyone else.”

However, systemic barriers persist, rooted in a Western pathological framework shaped by eugenic logic (Botha & Cage, 2022; Woods et al., 2018). CHY critiqued what we termed neuronormative hegemony, placing the burden of integration on autistic individuals in a neurotypical world:

“For autistic individuals to integrate into society and gain an equal perspective... I must constantly suppress my nature and pay a ‘behavioral tax’ for social citizenship of a ‘normal person’ and be treated as one.”

Here, normalcy operates as a gatekeeper, demanding autistic conformity while ignoring structural failures. The dominant paradigm prioritizes correcting individual defects over addressing systemic exclusion, disproportionately placing the burden on autistic individuals. Yet, autistic individuals are resisting this medicalized framework, rejecting “the violence of diagnosis and its restrictive consequences.” (PIPPI)

Instead, autistic advocates call for a paradigm shift from curing to supporting autonomy (Hegu, 2025). FLOWER emphasized:

“We must move from ‘curing disease’ to providing tailored support while

DISH WASHER further challenged society’s neurotypical-centric rules, arguing that social integration requires systemic change:

“I dislike the idea of social integration because both neurodivergent and neurotypical people think independently. Even compromise might be difficult for both sides to adapt to unless societal rules change.”

Through these critiques, autistic individuals assert themselves not as passive recipients of societal norms but as active agents driving transformative change.

Autistic individuals are reshaping homogenized autism discourse in the Chinese context through advocacy and actions. As PIPPI stated, “The core of destigmatization is not about reaching a singular consensus, but ultimately about achieving a better quality of life.” CHY echoed this, advocating for diverse approaches:

“On the path toward depathologizing terminology…we can choose our

## The benefits of reflecting, deconstructing, and reconstructing terminology

Reflecting on and redefining autism-related terminology enhances both personal growth and social integration. However, dialoguing with autism terminology in the Chinese context poses unique challenges compared to Western Indo-European linguistic contexts. These challenges include dual translations (孤独症 vs. 自闭症), uncertainties regarding the semantic nuances of $G u D u$ and ZiBi, inherent pathologization, and tensions with the English term “autism spectrum disorder.” Fig. 5 demonstrates a typical reflection process. Yet, this interplay enables autistic individuals to reshape their self-narratives, compare cross-linguistic concepts, and engage in more inclusive dialogues—ultimately enhancing their mental health and overall well-being and achieving what Chinese philosophy advocates: inner and outer harmony (Li & Cui, 2022).

For many, a formal diagnosis is just the beginning. SALTED FISH mentioned that the diagnosis label explained her struggles:

“I thought it would solve everything I saw hope. It explains my troubles:

Why am I depressed? What is this feeling of alienation?”

However, diagnostic labels also bring her uncertainty and stigma:

“Many issues still require step-by-step solutions. A diagnosis or its absence can be deeply unsettling, especially for adults newly exploring the label of ASD. Many will question: ‘Am I really on the spectrum, or am I just imagining it?’ Even after diagnosis, doubts linger ‘Did I just act like an autistic during the consultation?’”

Participants need to further refine their understanding of autism by contrasting initially familiar with ZBZ may associate it with negative stereotypes. However, through reflection, they may explore the English origin of autism, compare it with terms like GDZ or PuXi Zhang’Ai (spectrum disorder), and recognize their cultural and social nuances.

WISDOM, a parent of an autistic child, initially felt overwhelmed by the diagnosis: “Seeing the term ‘autism’ initially terrified me—nonverbal, socially detached, intellectually disabled...”

However, exposure to terms like spectrum (emphasizing diversity) and disorder prompted her to reevaluate her understanding. This shift not only helped her accept her child’s diagnosis but also led to personal introspection:

“[M]y perspective on autism changed fundamentally because I’m also on the spectrum. Even raising a child on the spectrum has become much less stressful for me.”

This illustrates how linguistic deconstruction can shift perspectives from a singular pathological view to a more inclusive, human-centered understanding.

Beyond individual empowerment, reconstructing autism-related terminology lays the foundation for a more inclusive society. Future research should refine this process, amplifying autistic voices to foster a more nuanced and globally relevant neurodiversity discourse. ## Individual level (intra-personal)

At the individual level, reflecting on and redefining autism-related terminology enhances self-awareness, allowing autistic individuals to articulate their experiences and develop a personalized understanding of autism.

## Expressing and articulating personal life experiences

By engaging with different terms, autistic individuals clarify their feelings and experiences. DISH WASHER shared how autism terminology helped her make sense of her identity:

“I love discovering book characters who remind me of myself and trying to emulate how they oversee life’s challenges. When I read about a protagonist on the spectrum, I immediately connected with him. [...] [A]fter experiencing four social setbacks in one week, I realized I was fundamentally different from others. That was my first real understanding of autism. Later, while answering an autism questionnaire, I felt relief its questions reflected my genuine

experiences.”

Developing personal understanding of autism

By deconstructing pathologizing terms, autistic individuals reshape their cognitive

frameworks, moving beyond stigma and reclaiming their narratives. PIPPI reflected:

“Years after my diagnosis, my understanding has evolved. The label no longer

overwhelms me. Now, I grapple with the constraints imposed by diagnosis and

my discomfort with existing narratives.”

For many, media representations are their first introduction to autism. When media autistic individuals must reevaluate their understanding:

“I once thought only those portrayed in media nonverbal, unresponsive,

Identifying and rationalizing personal needs and strengths

Reconstructing terminology helps autistic individuals recognize their strengths

By understanding their needs and abilities, autistic individuals can assert their rights and make meaningful societal contributions.

## Social and community levels (inter-personal)

At a broader level, reflecting on terminology fosters social integration, strengthens identity, and challenges stigma.

Accessing and navigating adaptive services

On the one hand, diagnostic labels can function as gateways for autistic individuals to access essential services. For example, JADE’s current position necessitates a disability certificate. More importantly, through deconstructing and redefining terminology, autistic individuals clarify their relationships with the autism labels, gain insight into their needs, and engage in more effective communications with the external WASHER described how she navigates social expectations:

“I only socialize through shared interests it’s enjoyable. Otherwise, it’s exhausting. I don’t want to conform to neurotypical-default social rules.”

Establishing one’s identity within the autistic community

Reconstructing terminology enables autistic individuals to connect with likeminded community members, define their roles, and cultivate a sense of belonging. CABBAGE highlighted her role:

“I function as a bridge between neurotypicals and more ‘severely’ autistic individuals. I can articulate what they (autistics) might struggle to express,

reflecting on our shared traits.”

Using autism as a meaningful communication label

The label of autism serves as precise language, enabling autistic individuals to explain their traits to others. For example, WISDOM shared her successful experiences in applying autism and ADHD labels to communicate meaningfully in the workplace: “I talk to close colleagues about autism. They’ve come to understand it better and now they’re even helping guide parents who have autistic kids. One coworker, whom I recognized as having ADHD, later identified a student exhibiting autistic traits.”

Challenging and resisting external stigma or accusations

Deeper engagement with autism terminology empowers individuals to counter societal misconceptions. WISDOM used a powerful analogy:

“Autism and ADHD are like genetic minorities. Most people are Han Chinese;

some are Tibetans or Mongolians. Would you feel ashamed of being an ethic

minority? No. So why do you need to feel ashamed of being on the spectrum?

It’s just genetic diversity.”

By reclaiming the authority to define and interpret the autism-related terms through their own narratives, autistic individuals uphold their dignity while fostering broader societal acceptance and inclusion.

## Discussion

As researchers of marginalized and multi-disciplinary groups, grounding our work in Eastern epistemologies empowers autistic individuals in China to reclaim their moral and political agency. This approach deepens our understanding of their lived experiences while providing a platform for self-expression, identity reconstruction, and social repositioning. By integrating epistemic injustice theory, counterstory theory, neurodiversity theory, and traditional Chinese philosophical concepts, this study develops a multifaceted framework for analyzing and reconstructing autism discourse in China. Beyond uncovering the sociocultural mechanisms behind terminology equitable social environments. Notably, incorporating traditional Chinese values demonstrates how global neurodiversity movements can be adapted to local contexts, proposing situated reworkings of neurodiversity that resist universalizing models.

Our findings reveal that autistic participants seek terminology that acknowledges their challenges without defining them solely through a medicalized lens (RQ1). The Chinese term for autism—particularly the suffix “-zhèng(症)”—inherently carries clinical and pathological connotations, prompting many participants to favor extended terminologies over the short form to “mitigate the sense of illness” (no.725, parent). In particular, our study reveals that smaller proportions of Chinese autistic individuals prefer the short form of autism [GDZ (69.2%), ZBZ (15.9%)], while higher proportions favor the extended labels [GDZ spectrum disorder (87.0%), ZBZ spectrum disorder (30.2%)] (see Supplementary Figure 1 & Supplementary Material 5).

This preference for longer, elaborated labels stands in stark contrast to findings from English- and French-using autistic individuals. Studies by Keating et al. (2023) and Geelhand et al. (2023) reported that participants overwhelmingly preferred the concise term “autism” (92.8% and 90.6%, respectively) over the full-length “autism spectrum disorder” (60.7% and 74.1%).

The divergence highlights how cultural context and perceived stigma—not just linguistic brevity—influence terminology preferences. In the Chinese context, the character “-zhèng (症)” evokes notions of disease and defect, making even officially endorsed terms feel pathologizing. Extending the label may thus function as a discursive strategy to soften its pathological weight and create conceptual space for identity negotiation.

This medicalized framing likely contributes to the low rates of diagnostic disclosure observed in our sample: fewer than 20% of parents reported having disclosed the diagnosis to their autistic children, while over 40% indicated they had no intention of doing so (see also Table S7). As Miller (2020, p. 94), an autistic scholar, critically observes:

“[I]f one has an autism label in China, this is normally not perceived in a positive way due to the deeper aspects of Chinese culture. It is also due to Chinese culture that some parents and autistic children will not fully embrace the autism label. [...] [M]any Chinese people see this (autistic identity) as a ‘weakness’.”

However, our in-depth conversations with autistic individuals highlight their active engagement in constructing diverse and coherent autism identity narratives through iterative dialogues with terminology (RQ2). This continuous negotiation— deconstructing and reconstructing language—proves essential for their well-being. In China, forming an autism identity requires navigating both Western hegemonic terminology and localized social metaphors. This complex identity-building process warrants greater attention, underscoring the need for further research and support for autistic individuals’ engagement with terminology.

Traditional Chinese philosophy emphasizes harmony in diversity, advocating for the coexistence of differences within a balanced whole (Li & Cui, 2022). This aligns with the neurodiversity theory, which frames autism as an expression of human diversity rather than a disorder to be eradicated. By integrating this philosophical perspective, we can shift the discourse from a deficit-based model to one that values autistic individuals’ unique contributions to society.

Throughout this study, we reconsidered our initial assumption that preferences for autism terminology in China could be reduced to binary choices. This oversimplified the complex social reality surrounding these terms. While we continue to recommend that official publications and media prioritize GDZ or GDZ spectrum disorder over ZBZ—incorporating concepts voiced by autistic individuals—we now recognize the importance of revisiting traditional Chinese philosophy for a more holistic approach.

Dialectical thinking, a core tenet of traditional Chinese philosophy, offers a valuable framework for resolving terminology debates such as GDZ versus ZBZ (Chai et al., 2024; X. Jiang, 2013). Rather than treating these terms as mutually exclusive, dialectical reasoning encourages viewing them as complementary aspects of the same phenomenon. This approach moves beyond simplistic dichotomies, fostering a more inclusive and contextually relevant autism discourse. By embracing this perspective, we align with neurodiversity principles while respecting the cultural richness of the Chinese context.

This study has several limitations. First, our focus on regions using Simplified Chinese limits the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, all participants were internet users, excluding perspectives from those without digital access. We also acknowledge that our questionnaire did not allow participants to rank terms “equally” based on non-preference; this option was omitted because the online survey platform does not support such functionality for ranking questions, though participants could choose not to respond to these items. Given that language evolves (Sankoff, 1980), our analysis reflects a specific temporal context. Future research should adopt longitudinal methodologies to track linguistic shifts. Nonetheless, our study spanned over a year and a half, employing multiple methods to triangulate data and iteratively interpret findings in collaboration with the autistic community. This approach strengthens the validity of our conclusions (Smith, 2018).

## Conclusion

In summary, by emphasizing autistic individuals’ narrative and moral agency while integrating insights from traditional Chinese thought, this study seeks to transcend Western hegemonic discourse and propose a research framework rooted in

China’s cultural and social realities. Future research should further localize autism discourses, fostering equal dialogues between Eastern and Western knowledge systems to advance a more inclusive global academic environment. Policymakers should also prioritize the needs of the autistic community by implementing policies that support their integration and self-actualization. Ultimately, we aim to amplify autistic voices, empower them with greater autonomy, and support their place in society. This not only reflects fundamental respect for autistic individuals but also contributes to social harmony. As Buddhist thought teaches, all beings —despite their differences—are fundamentally equal. Chinese Buddhist Venerable Master Hsing Yun (2007, p. 67) emphasized:

“Real equality must be based on the larger respecting the smaller, the many respecting the few, the strong respecting the weak, and the rich respecting the poor. Of course, a sense of equality towards all things can become a natural habit for us, and under such a concept of equality, the world will certainly have peace.’”

By embracing the philosophy of equality towards all things, we can foster a society that values diversity while maintaining harmony, ensuring that autistic individuals are recognized not as anomalies but as integral members of the human community.
