# Discourse-based approaches to autistic focussed interests: Understanding shared focus, mutual accommodation, and multimodal expression

Jessica Aiston1, \*, , Nelya Koteyko1 , and Martine van Driel2

1 Queen Mary University of London, London, United Kingdom

2 University of Birmingham, Birmingham, United Kingdom

\* Corresponding author. Queen Mary University of London, Mile End Road, London E1 4NS, United Kingdom. E-mail: j.aiston@qmul.ac.uk

Interactions between autistic and non-autistic people are often characterized by misunderstanding and a ‘double empathy problem’ (Milton, Gurbuz, and López 2022). Thus, there is a need for applied linguistics research that seeks to concretely address and improve these communication barriers and lead to more positive and equitable interactions. In this position paper, we critically review existing discourse approaches to autistic communication and argue that future research must be participatory, affrmative of neurodiversity, and applied in service of addressing this double empathy problem. In particular, we argue that applied linguists should pay more attention to the role of focussed interests and show how discourse analysis can shed light on the valuable role of interest-driven interactions, both among autistic people and between autistic and non-autistic people. Following this, we propose several directions for future directions for applied linguistics research into autistic communication. Specifcally, we suggest that linguistics could explore the potential for focussed interests for fostering shared focus, mutual accommodation, and multimodal expression, and consider how these fndings can be translated into positive impact for autistic communities.

## Introduction

Autism is characterized by ‘differences to the nonautistic population in several domains, including social and cognitive style, and sensory processing’ (Pearson and Rose 2021: 53). Characteristics associated with autism include diffculties with social communication, sensitivity to sensory phenomena, and highly focussed interests (National Autistic Society n. d.). One approach to understanding autism is monotropism, an interest-based account which posits that ‘in autism, processing resource strongly tends to localize and concentrate to the exclusion of other input’ (Murray 2018: 1). This means having fewer interests aroused at a given time, but these interests are felt more strongly, and it is diffcult to focus attention elsewhere once one has begun engaging with an interest. In this view, autism is conceptualized as a different or ‘neurodivergent’ (i.e.

different from the dominant or ‘neurotypical’) way of processing and experiencing the world and not a defcient or disordered one. Within a ‘neurodiversity’ framework, we recognize the great diversity in ‘cognitive, affectual, and sensory functioning’, among the general population (Bertilsdotter Rosqvist, Chown, and Stenning 2020) and critique the ableist, neuro-normative paradigm that positions autism (and other forms of neurodivergence such as ADHD) as substandard.

In line with the neurodiversity framework is the double empathy problem, defned as a ‘breakdown in mutual understanding’ that may occur when ‘people of very differing dispositions attempt to interact’ (Milton, Gurbuz, and López 2022: 1901). An autistic and allistic (i.e. non-autistic) person may experience communication barriers due to differences in processing styles, communication preferences, and life experiences. These communication diffculties have been primarily attributed to impairments of the autistic individual, rather than failures of mutual understanding. Such assumptions have been based on the defcit approach, which has suggested that autistic populations lack theory of mind and are incapable of social connection (Baron-Cohen, Leslie, and Frith 1985). However, experimental studies have shown that allistic people are also unsuccessful at interpreting the mental states of autistic people (Edey et al. 2016). In addition, intra-autistic communication has been shown to be equally successful as intraallistic communication (Crompton et al. 2020; Williams, Wharton, and Jagoe 2021), demonstrating a clear existence of autistic communication styles.

These fndings are in line with Ochs and Solomon’s (2010: 70) ‘autistic sociality’, which builds upon the assumption that ‘human sociality consists of a range of possibilities for social coordination with others’. Dominant, neurotypical understandings of sociality may not adequately capture the nature of social activities within autistic communities. For example, in autistic spaces there is greater acceptance of ‘info-dumping’, that is, sharing great volumes of information about an interest (Long 2024). Bertilsdotter Rosqvist (2019) distinguishes between interest-based sociality and socially based sociality. Interest-based sociality, preferred in autistic spaces, entails that interactions and exchanges are based on, and motivated by, a genuine interest in a topic. Conversely, socially based sociality, based on phatic communion and small talk, may be exhausting or serve little purpose in autistic spaces. Findings across these studies demonstrate the existence of an autistic communication style and highlight the need for further investigation into underresearched authentic autistic interactions.

Today, interest-driven interactions increasingly take place online as people join social media groups or ‘affnity spaces’ dedicated to specifc interests. As defned by Gee (2004), ‘affnity spaces’ are loosely organized places (online or offine) built around shared passions, goals, and practices, rather than shared demographic characteristics. Newcomers and experts alike participate in the same space and learn from each other, and there are many ways of participating and gaining status. Different forms of knowledge are valued and encouraged, including intensive knowledge about a specialist topic and extensive knowledge about a variety of subjects. Consequently, affnity spaces may be suited for those with a monotropic processing style.

However, interest-driven interactions are notably absent from the literature on autism and communication. There are few studies that approach autistic interests from a discourse or linguistic perspective and even fewer that approach the topic within the neurodiversity framework. In this discussion paper, we advocate discourse approaches to autistic interest-driven communication in naturalistic settings such as online affnity spaces. As we will demonstrate, discourse approaches have been important for providing insight into autistic communication, emphasizing the necessity of a situated approach, and shifting the focus from defcits to differences. We argue that there is a pressing need for research that aims to build on this previous work and tangibly address the double empathy problem, facilitating successful cross-neurotype interaction. Applied linguistics should explore the possibility of mutually rewarding, interest-driven interactions and seek to translate empirical fndings into positive outcomes—for example, by creating research-informed tools to help develop inclusive online spaces and promote acceptance of diverse communication styles. Just as our stance is informed by long-term collaborations with autistic communities (Koteyko et al. 2023), we argue that such future applied linguistics research must be participatory in nature.

The paper is structured as follows. First, we provide background on focussed interests and autism. Next, we review existing discourse approaches to autistic communication, both generally and in the context of focussed interests. We argue that discourse approaches to autistic communication on focussed interests can help us understand, and eventually foster, shared focus, mutual understanding, and multimodal expression in interactions between autistic and allistic people. As such, they can help overcome the double empathy problem and promote more equitable interactions. Finally, we outline areas for future research and propose several discourse analysis frameworks that can be used to study interest-driven communication.

Throughout this paper, we use identity-frst language (i.e. autistic person instead of person with autism), to align with UK community preferences (Kenny et al. 2016). We use focussed or intense interests as opposed to other terms given within the literature such as circumscribed or restricted interests. As Wood (2021: 35) argues, the latter terms can imply ‘impairment and dysfunction’, or that autistic interests are a ‘problematic trait which must be prevented or remediated’, which is not a stance we endorse.

## Background: focussed interests in autism

It is estimated that between 65 per cent and 95 per cent of autistic people have at least one area of highly intense interest (Turner-Brown et al. 2011; Grove et al. 2018). In line with monotropism, autistic interests may differ from neurotypical interests in terms of their specifcity and level of intensity (Turner-Brown et al. 2011; Anthony et al. 2013; Long 2024). For example, one may spend almost 40 hours a week engaging with an intense interest (Bross, Huffman, and Hagiwara 2022). Studies have often approached the topic from a defciency perspective and characterized autistic interests as excessive, odd, or obstructive. For example, studies have involved measuring the degree of ‘interference’ posed by focussed interests (Turner-Brown et al. 2011; Anthony et al. 2013). As Wood (2021) argues, these framings are pathologizing and harmful. Due to stigmatization and bullying, autistic people report having to hide their interests from others or refraining from talking about their interests in social or work situations (Bross, Huffman, and Hagiwara 2022). However, such ‘masking’ practices (i.e. (sub)consciously modifying behaviour in order to appear ‘less autistic’) are associated with negative mental and physical health outcomes, including burnout, anxiety, and even suicidality (Pearson and Rose 2021).

Despite these pejorative framings, there are many positive aspects of focussed interests. They are often an integral part of someone’s identity (Long 2024) and may be experienced as a source of pride (Winter-Messiers 2007). Engaging with interests can have a positive impact on wellbeing (Grove et al. 2018; Bross, Huffman, and Hagiwara 2022) and self-image (Winter-Messiers 2007), alleviating feelings of anxiety (Koenig and Williams 2017). In addition, focussed interests can aid with the development of specialist skills (Winter-Messiers 2007), improve engagement with school curricula (Wood 2021), and act as a pathway into employment or education opportunities (Koenig and Williams 2017). A reappraisal of focussed interests may, therefore, lead to positive outcomes, such as increased rates of employment and educational attainment. Such an enterprise would align with UK government initiatives to boost the prospects of autistic adults, as currently only 3 in 10 are in employment (Department for Work and Pensions 2023).

Although focussed interests are sometimes characterized as ‘non-social’ (Turner-Brown et al. 2011), these studies are not based on notions of autistic sociality. Shared interests can help motivate an individual to initiate and sustain interactions with others (Koenig and Williams 2017; Stone, Mills, and Saggers 2019) and may form the basis of friendships (Lizon, Tales, and Vanheule 2024). Autistic adults interviewed by Lizon, Tales, and Vanheule (2024) said they enjoyed talking about their interests with others and that intense interests could help to ‘make sense’ of a social situation by providing a structure or framework to follow. Likewise, Cross et al. (2024) found that autistic board game fans experienced social interactions during gaming sessions as easier and lower pressure, as the board game provides a central focus and conversation occurs in parallel with the game. Playing board games together could also assist with the development of specifc communication skills. This illustrates the importance of focussed interests for fostering shared focus and mutual engagement in both intra-neurotype and cross-neurotype communication situations.

Finally, researchers have observed that when the topic changes to an area of interest, autistic interactants may display linguistic and interactional changes that signal reduced masking and communication diffculty. For example, Winter-Messiers (2007: 147) observed heightened affect, improved ‘intelligibility’ and ‘sophistication of their vocabulary, word order, and syntax’ as well as ‘improvement in body language’ in terms of increased eye contact and expressive gestures. More recently, Wood (2021) found that school pupils talked for longer, initiated more spontaneous interactions, and had less diffculty answering questions. Similarly, teachers interviewed by Stone, Mills, and Saggers (2019: 218) remarked that students ‘did not have to “worry about” their gestures’, and ‘the pressure to make eye contact was lost’.

In sum, we have demonstrated that shared interests are an important tool for sociality and communication, refecting notions of interest-based sociality. Focussed interests are important for establishing shared focus and common ground between interactants, who can align with each other based on objects of mutual enjoyment. In interest-based exchanges, interactants may exhibit improved social motivation and communication skills, reduced feelings of anxiety or stress, and realize opportunities for mutual understanding. In order to build on this previous work, we argue that in order to promote acceptance of diverse communication styles and facilitate equitable cross-neurotype exchanges, future applied linguistics research must consider the role of focussed interests. In the subsequent sections, we review existing discourse approaches to autistic communication, followed by discourse approaches to focussed interests.

## Discourse-based studies of autistic communication

Discourse analysis can be broadly defned as the study of how semiotic systems ‘affect the kinds of meanings people can make in different situations, the kinds of actions they can perform, the kinds of relationships they can form, and the kinds of people they can be’, based on the assumption that discourse plays a crucial role in the mediation, reproduction, and transmission of social practices (Jones et al. 2015: 4). Discourse analysis is not confned to the study of the written or spoken word but all semiotic modes in which meaning can be created, such as gestures, facial expressions, colours, images, or music (Jewitt 2016). Furthermore, as Goodwin (2000; 2018) argues, language and context cannot be treated as strictly separate. Instead, social action involves the simultaneous deployment of a range of semiotic resources that mutually elaborate one another.

By contrast, defcit approaches to autistic communication assume that language can be studied independently of context, that language is an objective way of representing experience, and that social and linguistic norms can be determined a priori. Observed features of autistic communication are interpreted as ‘symptomatic … direct manifestations of defcits’ (Sterponi and De Kirby 2016: 394). For example, topic perseveration (especially about areas of focussed interest) is typically considered a pragmatic defcit stemming from impaired presupposition skills (e.g. Paul et al. 2009). However, discourse analysis necessitates a situated approach, in which language is conceptualized as a form of social action and an interactional accomplishment of multiple interlocutors (Goodwin 1979, 2000). Thus, analysts examine how meaning is jointly created and negotiated through interaction. Features of autistic communication should therefore be interpreted as fulflling strategic, interactional functions (Sterponi and De Kirby 2016; Bottema-Beutel 2017; Maciejewska 2019). To demonstrate, Stribling et al. (2009) conducted a conversation analysis of an interaction between an autistic child ‘Chris’, an allistic researcher, and a robot. Although Chris often circled back to the same topic, the repetition was always in response to changes in the local environment and directed towards the interlocutor, who continually treated the topic as relevant. Rather than obsessive self-talk about his specifc interest area, the authors suggested that topic perseveration was a reasonable conversational strategy and a ‘complex, collaborative activity’ (Stribling et al. 2009: 577). Therefore, we argue that discourse analysis can provide a more comprehensive picture of autistic interest-driven communication.

Furthermore, the double empathy problem theorizes that communication barriers arise due to failures of mutual misunderstanding rather than failures of autistic cognition (Milton, Gurbuz, and López 2022). Studies have shown that groups of autistic people transmit information more accurately (Crompton et al. 2020) and build better rapport (Williams, Wharton, and Jagoe 2021) than groups with mixed neurotypes. Discourse approaches can provide further insight into this creation of rapport and intersubjectivity. For example, Heasman and Gillespie (2019: 915) observed interactions during mutual video game play at an autism support group and noted ‘generous’ assumptions of common ground and frequent ‘sudden and specifc’ topic shifts. Although this could be considered as dysfunctional by neurotypical standards, here these features allowed for creativity and humour. Similarly, Williams, Wharton, and Jagoe (2021: 17) found that conversations between autistic interactants had a greater sense of ‘fow, rapport and intersubjective attunement’ compared to their interactions with allistic interactants. The authors explained these results with reference to monotropism and relevance theory: because both participants have similar monotropic processing styles, mutual understanding is easier and more cognitive resources are available for language production.

Discourse analysis can also show distinctive features and values upon which autistic communication is based. For instance, Koskinen, Stevanovic, and Peräkylä (2021) investigated differences in the face management strategies of autistic and allistic interactants. While autistic participants acknowledged face threats towards their interlocutor, they did not engage in as many face-saving actions. This shows that while autistic populations understand face, they may place higher importance on authenticity than preserving social status. Therefore, we recommend that future studies re-consider positive face to be more inclusive of autistic interactional norms and characteristics. Moreover, the ‘Autistic Adults Online’ project combined discourse frameworks with digital ethnography to explore distinctive features of online autistic communication (Koteyko et al. 2023). In one study, Van Driel and Koteyko (2022) investigated speech acts within the replies of autistic Twitter1 users. The most frequent speech act categories included Claim, Inform, and Elaborate, with Answer and Qualify being used when talking about autism. Such direct, information-focussed replies were not impolite, but related to ‘autistic preference for direct and clear interactions, and reliance on question and answers' (Van Driel and Koteyko 2022: 15). Next, Koteyko et al. (2024) used corpus-assisted discourse analysis to examine linguistic strategies of stigma management, fnding that those who did not disclose autism were more likely to use mitigation in their social media posts. In follow-up interviews, these participants reported feelings of anxiety over being misunderstood and consequently tried to carefully phrase their posts. In contrast, the social media posts of users who disclosed autism included words indexing autistic identity or affliation with autistic communities (e.g. autistic, people, #AskingAutistics) more frequently. These participants also highlighted autistic sociality and communicative preferences during the interview.

From this review, we argue that discourse approaches can provide important insights into autistic sociality. Importantly, discourse approaches do not entail a dismissal of areas of diffculty but rather facilitate a shift in focus ‘from defcits to abilities’ (Maciejewska 2019: 313) and an acknowledgement that these diffculties are ‘differentially manifested’ and ‘often set up by the interlocutors’ (Sterponi and De Kirby 2016: 403). Communication barriers may be reduced when interacting in a comfortable or structured setting or with someone of the same neurotype, or when the conversation relates to a topic of focussed interest. These fndings are important for raising awareness of autistic communication preferences. Future work should build on this by promoting not just awareness but acceptance of diverse communication styles and facilitating accommodating interactions. In the following section, we review existing discourse-based studies of autistic communication and consider the necessity of a neurodiverse-affrmative stance when conducting this work.

## Focussed interests from a discourse perspective

So far, we have established that communication may be easier or more enjoyable when the topic relates to an area of intense interest. Some researchers have also observed changes in communication when discussing focussed interests, such as lengthier turns or improved motivation to initiate or sustain interactions (Winter-Messiers 2007; Stone, Mills, and Saggers 2019; Wood 2021). However, this tends to be based on general observations rather than systematic linguistic analysis.

There are two notable studies that have used conversation analysis frameworks to study interest-driven communication. First, Dean, Adams, and Kasari (2013) conducted a conversation analysis of narrative-based interactions between ‘Cindy’ (an autistic girl aged 7) and her allistic peers. Cindy produced almost as many narratives as the rest of the girls combined and these narratives often related to her focussed interest. However, her narratives tended to be rejected and met with silence, requests to change topic, and mockery. We contend that this refects Bottema-Beutel’s (2017: 29) assertion that autistic people tend to be ‘socially situated in positions where their interactive contributions are either not taken up, or are quite constrained’. Furthermore, the fact that Cindy persevered in telling narratives, despite recognizing her peers’ resistance, demonstrates the need for inclusive affnity spaces where interests can be discussed in depth.

Next, Aizaki, Walton, and Lewis (2023) conducted a multimodal conversation analysis of a discussion between an autistic man ‘Michael’ and an allistic woman ‘Susan’ and analysed changes in the conversational fow and use of gestures when the topic changed to Michael’s area of interest. When describing his preferred board games, Michael adopted a strong epistemic and affective stance (cf. Koteyko et al. 2024) and his turns included a high level of detail, presenting himself as knowledgeable and passionate. Michael also showed an increased speech rate, while his gestures were ‘frequent and exaggerated, relative to earlier in the interaction’ (Aizaki, Walton, and Lewis 2023: 93). However, the authors observed ‘asymmetries in the number and duration of turns at talk’ as Michael was ‘dominating’ the interaction (Aizaki, Walton, and Lewis 2023: 96) while Susan provided minimal responses and showed non-verbal signs of disengagement, such as averting her eyes.

These studies highlight distinctive features of autistic interest-driven communication, such as a high level of detail and topic perseveration, as well as potential barriers to successful crossneurotype interaction. However, we argue that future research must be more affrmative of neurodiversity and considerate of the double empathy problem. The lack of conversational fow noted by Aizaki, Walton, and Lewis (2023: 96) was mainly attributed to Michael and his ‘little sensitivity to the asymmetries he has created’ with only small consideration of Susan’s role in the interaction. Similarly, Dean, Adams, and Kasari (2013) posited interventions to help Cindy construct more appealing narratives, rather than how her peers could support Cindy. Instead, future discourse research should aim to improve communication outcomes by facilitating mutually rewarding and benefcial interest-driven exchanges.

For instance, applied linguists could assist with the creation or development of online affnity spaces where users can engage in lengthy exchanges about topics of intense interest or provide guidance on how neurotypical users can better support autistic users in those interactions. Importantly, this research must be neurodiversity-affrmative and refrain from evaluating autistic communication against neurotypical standards. For example, presenting increased levels of eye contact when discussing interests as an example of ‘improved’ communication skills (Winter-Messiers 2007) could suggest that autistic preferences for decreased eye contact are less valid and promote harmful masking processes.

## Future directions

In the fnal part of this paper, we propose the following areas for future directions in discourse-based research into autistic interest-driven communication: shared focus, mutual accommodation, and multimodal expression. Before we explore this further, there are considerations that are relevant for all applied linguists studying autistic communication regardless of topic, framework, or methodology.

First, linguists must acknowledge the epistemic injustice stemming from the fact that most knowledge about autistic communication has been produced by allistic researchers, without consultation or collaboration with autistic people. One way of addressing this issue is through participatory research and co-production, including leadership by autistic researchers or partnership with autistic community members (Fletcher-Watson et al. 2019). For example, researchers could collaborate with community members when devising research questions and interpreting data. By affrming the expertise derived from lived experiences and ensuring that research objectives align with the actual needs of the autistic population, participatory models have the potential to produce impactful, empowering research. This can also improve research quality, as autistic researchers and collaborators can build a strong rapport with participants and provide insight that would otherwise be missed (Pellicano et al. 2022). However, co-production is uncommon within linguistics (see Williams, Wharton, and Jagoe 2021; Koteyko et al. 2023 for exceptions). Although fully participatory research may not always be possible, we argue that applied linguists should take seriously the ideals of coproduction going forward. Similarly, research into online communication must follow ethical procedures and consider how to navigate issues of privacy/publicity, informed consent, anonymity, and representation. A full discussion of ethical principles is beyond the scope of this paper, but we direct readers to discussions in Spilioti and Tagg (2017) or Bertilsdotter Rosqvist and Jackson-Perry (2024).

Second, applied linguists must strive towards intersectionality and inclusion. Despite comprising a large section of the autistic population, autistic people who are non-speaking, low-speaking, and/or have a co-occurring intellectual disability are regularly excluded both as participants and as researchers (Fletcher-Watson et al. 2019), as are Black women and girls (Lovelace et al. 2022). Instead, we must aim for increased diversity in our samples, advisory boards, and research teams and crucially, ensure this representation is not merely tokenistic. Furthermore, we argue that linguistics research into autistic communication must be intersectional in nature. As Milton, Gurbuz, and López (2022) highlight, the double empathy problem does not only apply to cross-neurotype interactions. Therefore, it is important to consider the intersection of ableist norms with racist, sexist, and Anglocentric linguistic norms and acknowledge that autism is never the only facet of someone’s identity. For example, applied linguists should explore how autistic interactants with different ethnicities, genders, or linguistic backgrounds, create intersubjectivity or experience communication barriers.

Finally, we agree with Stenning (2024: x) that it is necessary for researchers to ‘recognize the autistic subject’s capacity for critical resistance and reconstruction’, and pay attention to those who ‘don’t recapitulate oppressive norms or the opinions of more powerful others’. For example, Kim and Bottema-Beutel (2019) employed narrative discourse analysis to explore how autistic bloggers challenged hegemonic, medicalized ideologies about stimming2 and reframed it as a positive, healthy activity. While the role of oppressive ableist norms should not be ignored, we suggest that linguists also analyse social and discursive practices which resist those norms, as well as how autistic communities negotiate meaning-making, construct collective or individual identities, and enact autistic socialities.

## Shared focus

Shared focus is a key feature of autistic interest-driven interactions. Murray (2018: 2) points out that rather than trying to guess what another person is thinking and ‘using language effectively to manipulate others’ interest systems’, connecting over shared experiences and interests is about ‘a freedom of shared joy and wonder that entirely transcends self’. Indeed, Williams, Wharton, and Jagoe (2021) showed that autistic strangers achieved great conversational fow and rapport, particularly when the conversation turned to a topic of shared interest. To examine how such shared focus may be achieved in both intra- and cross-neurotype interactions, we suggest that researchers could draw on the work of Du Bois’ (2007) stance triangle and Martin and White’s (2005) appraisal framework.

First, researchers could examine shared focus through examination of stance. Stance can be approached as ‘a linguistically articulated form of social action’ (Du Bois 2007: 139). For Du Bois, stance is linked with dialogicality and intersubjectivity: stance-takers do not simply produce isolated expressions of opinion but draw on stances expressed previously and use stance to align with others. The stance triangle captures how stance-taking involves three simultaneous actions: evaluating an object, positioning a subject (usually the stance-taker), and aligning with other subjects who share the stance. Moreover, Biri (2023: 79) considers how, in the context of online interest-driven communication, stance-taking may be ‘mediated through a mutual hashtag’ where users broadcast their evaluation to unknown and unseen audiences who may share the interest and browse the same hashtag. Thus, Biri proposes extending the stance triangle for the study of online communication to encompass the ambient community.

Stance has been studied in the context of online fandom. Matley (2020) examined stancetaking practices in Instagram memorial posts made by David Bowie fans following the singer’s death, while Biri (2023) analysed stance verbs used by fans of One Piece (a Japanese media franchise) on Twitter, Tumblr, and Reddit. These studies highlighted the role of stance in constructing identities as authentic, knowledgeable, and engaged fans. Furthermore, the studies showcased how interactants align with each other through shared focus and evaluation over a third point, and in doing so form online communities of interest. Consequently, we propose that the stance triangle would be a useful tool for the examination of shared focus and could be extended to the study of online affnity spaces beyond media fandom.

Appraisal Theory is another valuable approach to studying affnity spaces and autistic interactions. Martin and White (2005) outline an approach to the study of appraisal and evaluation, which includes identifying systems of Attitude (expression of emotions or feelings), Engagement (positioning of speakers or writers), and Graduation (modifcation of the strength of evaluation). Attitude can be further divided into Affect (positive or negative emotion), Judgement (evaluation of human behaviour), and Appreciation (evaluation of objects or phenomena). Koteyko, Van Driel, and Vines (2022) used this framework to explore how autistic Twitter users signalled affliation and used evaluative stances to align with others. The high prevalence of positive Affect, Appreciation, and Judgement was found to ‘instantiate self-presentation practices characteristic of SNS such as evaluating and emphasizing aspects of one’s personality’, and challenge stereotypes of autistic individuals as ‘emotionally distant and socially avoidant’ (Koteyko, Van Driel, and Vines 2022: 397). Consequently, appraisal research has the potential to counter harmful stereotypes and misconceptions that autistic people inherently lack empathy or sociality. Instead, we can illuminate a diversity of autistic socialities and communicative strengths, promoting greater social acceptance.

Building on this work, we suggest that appraisal analysis could provide further insight into autistic sociality in the context of interest-driven communication, for example in terms of users’ Engagement with interest-based texts, Judgement of other users’ behaviour in the affnity space, or Appreciation of the value of interests and affnity spaces. Used in conjunction with Du Bois’ (2007) stance triangle, Appraisal research can also illustrate how users affliate and connect with each other in affnity spaces, for example, via shared appraisals of the same object, person, or text. Autistic interests are often pathologized or stigmatized and so many autistic people are pressured to modify or conceal their passions in order to ft in with others (Bross, Huffman, and Hagiwara 2022). By examining the ways in which focussed interests are (positively) appraised, linguists can emphasize their importance for wellbeing and self-development. Ultimately, this work could help de-pathologize focussed interests and ensure that parents, teachers, clinicians, and others who work with autistic people, come to view them not as a problematic symptom in need of intervention, but a positive value in need of encouragement.

## Mutual accommodation and understanding

Shared interests can help foster mutual understanding (Murray 2018) and ensure that interactants are equally engaged with each other. Communication Accommodation Theory (CAT), which ‘seeks to explain and predict when, how, and why individuals engage in interactional adjustments with others, as well as recipients’ inferences, attributions, and evaluations of, and responses to them’ (Giles, Edwards, and Walther 2023: 1), is one promising framework to examine crossneurotype interactions. During an interaction, an individual may more or less consciously adjust their communication patterns, including lexis, gestures, or prosodic features, to attune with their conversational partner and achieve some interactional goal. For example, one may seek to create intimacy with their interlocutor and thus modify their speech to sound more alike (convergence). Conversely, one may increase the social distance by accentuating perceived linguistic differences (divergence). These accommodation strategies may be noticed and judged by the interlocutor as successfully accommodative, non-accommodative, over-accommodative, or under-accommodative. Furthermore, accommodation strategies may be mediated by stereotypes (Coupland et al. 1988), as people may accommodate to stereotypes of their interlocutor and any groups to which they are perceived to belong, rather than to their interlocutor as an individual.

To demonstrate, Coupland et al. (1988) proposed a typology of cross-generational language strategies. For instance, they found that younger speakers would adjust their speech to be louder and slower when communicating with elderly speakers, even when their interlocutor did not have a hearing impairment. Such a strategy could be perceived as over-accommodating or patronizing. This typology could be adapted for cross-neurotype accommodation. Based on the double empathy problem and evidence that allistic people underestimate the social and cognitive capabilities of autistic people (Heasman and Gillespie 2018), we expect that cross-neurotype communication would involve forms of over- and under-accommodation. For example, an allistic interactant could overestimate an autistic person’s diffculties with phatic talk and avoid asking them any social questions but continue to pose such questions to others in the group. Although the allistic person may judge this as appropriately accommodative, the autistic person may feel singled out. Similarly, an autistic interactant may engage in masking practices due to fear of social judgement, but these strategies may be perceived as strange or excessive (e.g. making ‘too much’ eye contact, or sounding ‘too formulaic’ when employing a rehearsed social script).

Thus, we suggest that future work could apply CAT to interest-driven interactions in online affnity spaces or compare the accommodation within interest-based affnity spaces to accommodation on platforms based around socially based sociality (such as Facebook). Prioritizing interest-based sociality and exchanges about areas of focussed interests could already be an important accommodation strategy in itself. Following the argument of Murray (2018), we could anticipate that the accommodation would be more successful in an affnity space when the topic concerns intense interests and where monotropic communication styles (such topic perseveration or ‘info-dumping’ a large volume of detailed information) are the norm. Here, allistic interactants may engage in successful forms of convergence to accommodate and build rapport with autistic interactants in online affnity spaces.

Finally, we propose that the results of this research be applied to the development of practical resources for creating and developing inclusive online environments (see Koteyko et al. 2023). If accommodation is found to be frequent and successful, then these fndings could be used to showcase good practice and form models of optimal cross-neurotype interaction. On the other hand, if the accommodation is judged to be infrequent or unsuccessful then these fndings could inform guidelines for improvements and modifcations to online spaces so that they are more accommodative of diverse communication styles.

## Multimodal expression

Finally, we propose that discourse-based research into online interest-driven communication is well positioned to consider the role of semiotic modalities beyond language in fostering affliation in interest-based interactions. Social media and digital technologies offer new opportunities and potentials for multimodal expression (see Jewitt 2016), such as emojis, GIFs, and likes. Furthermore, as Henner argues, linguists must attend to the visual, graphemic, and tactile languages used by autistic communities (Henner and Robinson 2023; Henner 2024).

Many autistic people communicate using Alternative and Augmented Communication, Picture Exchange Communication Systems, and signed languages. Therefore, research into autistic interest-driven communication, in both online and offine settings, must not focus on written/ spoken language alone but consider multimodal, embodied forms of interaction.

One possibility is the multimodal realizations of stance and Appraisal, for example, through gestures in videos about focussed interests. However, the meaning and usage of gestures, postures, and facial expressions within autistic communities may differ from the neurotypical norm. For example, direct eye contact in TikTok videos has been analysed as conveying sincerity (Van Poucke 2023), but many autistic people prefer to avoid eye contact. Brewer et al. (2016) found that autistic emotional expressions were more poorly recognized than allistic expressions, while Faso, Sasson, and Pinkman (2015) found that they were recognized accurately but also judged as more intense and less natural. Consequently, developing a coding framework to analyse autistic emotional expression would require careful consideration, ideally consultation or co-production with the autistic community. An important outcome of such work would be dispelling harmful misconceptions that autistic individuals lack emotion, instead working to increase public awareness and acceptance of communication differences.

Next, Ochs and Solomon (2010) and Maciejewska (2019) discuss the potential of physical objects to mediate autistic social interaction. Video games and board games are frequent intense interest areas among autistic people and so researchers have considered their potential to facilitate successful social interactions (Stone, Mills, and Saggers 2019; Cross et al. 2024). For example, Heasman and Gillespie (2019) studied video game-mediated interactions at an autism support group, noting how the shared focus on a mediating object facilitated the creation of intersubjectivity and rapport. The fact that interactants could face the screen and not each other minimized diffculties with eye contact, while the multimedia gaming context allowed participants to draw on shared language and intertextual references. This shows how multimodal engagement with mediating objects of shared interest can facilitate mutual understanding. There is also a community angle here, as technologies allow users to follow and create new conventions in a community via multimodal tools. Goodwin (2018) explains that different communities inherit different resources and established members must teach new members how to skilfully use these resources. Thus, we suggest that linguists could explore how autistic individuals are socialized into affnity spaces and taught to harness mediating technologies, such as games. In particular, linguistics research may help inform the development of training resources for moderators or creators of online affnity spaces, so that they are better equipped to support their autistic users and scaffold newcomer’s interactions.

Beyond the focus on single objects or tools as mediators in interactions involving autistic people, multimodal discourse analysis can contribute to scholarly and activist efforts to shift attention away from innate cognition to a relational understanding of self. Stenning (2024: 9) proposes a concept of agency that ‘consists in the sense of ourselves, which we gain through our relationships with others and with ourselves, through time and in particular geographic locations’, and which is deployed ‘not only when we speak or write, but also when we act, gesture, or touch others’. As Goodwin (2000: 1492) argues, ‘the production and interpretation of human social action is built through the simultaneous deployment of a range of [...] semiotic resources’ and that talk gains power via ‘placement within larger sequential structures, encompassing activities, social structural arrangements, and participation frameworks’. Thus, the analytical focus is not on the individual action or cognition, but on the ‘interactive feld that is sustained through the cooperative action of different kinds of actors’ (Goodwin 2018). This includes attention to affordances for action available in a particular environment (Gibson 1979) and how they are (not) taken up as part of collaborative sense-making (Stenning 2024).

For example, Van Driel and Koteyko (2022) drew on the concept of affordances-in-practice (Costa 2018) to provide a situated account of autistic sociality on Twitter. Their analysis showed how affordance of ‘association’ was enacted through engagement with Autism-related hashtags (e.g. #AskingAutistics), polls, and a preference for replies over broadcast tweets (Van Driel and

Koteyko 2022). Such practices relate to autistic preferences for directness, information sharing, and structured interactions. Future work may build on this by examining the affordances taken up by autistic users in online affnity spaces. With this knowledge, linguists can help members and moderators understand what attracts autistic users to these spaces, as well as barriers to participation. Using this multimodal lens to digital multimodal practices, linguists can further challenge the assumption of absent social motivation ascribed to autistic people and create more accessible online spaces.

## Conclusion

In this discussion article, we have advocated the value of discourse approaches to the study of autistic interest-driven communication, particularly in online affnity spaces. Discourse-based frameworks, such as Appraisal Theory, the stance triangle, and CAT can be used to examine the role of shared focus, mutual understanding, and multimodal expression in the context of interest-driven interactions. As we have shown, this is important because interest-driven communication can help foster mutual engagement and accommodation (Murray 2018). Ultimately, we argue that such research should be employed in service of facilitating equitable interactions between autistic and allistic people, overcoming the double empathy problem.

Linguistic research has the potential to improve public understanding of autistic communication and counter harmful stereotypes of autistic individuals as incapable of interacting, connecting, or empathizing with others. Furthermore, linguistic research can help to raise public awareness and understanding of the importance of interest-driven communication in the lives of autistic people, dispelling misconceptions that focussed interests pose a barrier to ‘real’ socializing. Moreover, discourse-based research can provide important insight into interest-driven interactions, including how mutual understanding and alignment are achieved and identifying potential barriers to communication. In applying this work, we argue that research should go beyond merely describing autistic communication differences and instead move towards evidence-based applications for nurturing and scaffolding social interactions.

For example, while many social interventions aim at teaching communication ‘rules’ or social skills, autistic youth may beneft more substantially from participation in affnity spaces where they can connect with others based on mutual interests and communicate in natural, yet structured, settings. This is encapsulated by the words of ‘Ben’: ‘I am sick of social skills groups … Why can’t someone go to the bar with me or to chess club? That’s what I need’ (quoted in Bagatell 2007: 45). Following Koteyko et al. (2023), discourse research could be applied in the creation of resources for designing more inclusive affnity spaces so that everyone’s contributions are welcomed and accommodated. As Ito et al. (2018) outline, affnity spaces can foster ‘connected learning’ opportunities, that is the connection of personal interests to academic, civic, and career-related opportunities. Through the support of affnity spaces, autistic individuals may gain confdence and learn practical, transferable skills such as creative writing or programming. Thus, facilitating participation in such spaces would not only assist with social and communication opportunities but could also help to improve educational and employment outcomes for autistic people (see Department for Work and Pensions 2023).

Finally, while applied linguistics research should be oriented towards providing solutions (technological or otherwise) to communication barriers and improving outcomes for autistic people, we argue that it is equally important that we draw attention to how these barriers were initially constructed. Applied linguists must not only concern themselves with issues of inclusion, but justice for autistic and disabled populations and ‘critically examine the overarching social structures we are participating in, upholding, and creating anew with our work’ (Bennett and Keyes 2020: 39). We must recognize that linguistics research has contributed to the marginalization and disempowerment of autistic people, for example, through labelling autistic language as defcient or the promotion of speech therapies that enforce conformity to neurotypical standards (Henner 2024). Going forward, applied linguists must aim to not only affrm and validate diverse communication styles but also collaborate with critical autism studies scholars and nonacademic partners to dismantle those oppressive, ableist ideologies that position autistic communication as substandard in the frst place. As well as using the preferred language and terminology of autistic communities, where possible, our research should involve participatory methods in which knowledge is produced in collaboration with autistic researchers and community members. This requires recognition of the expertise that derives from lived experience and commitments to redress the imbalance of power so that knowledge about autism is no longer exclusively produced by allistic researchers. Furthermore, we as linguists must recognize intersectionality and ensure that studies do not focus only on the most privileged language users—instead, highlighting the creative, agentive, and multimodal practices of the entire autism spectrum.

## Positionality and limitations

We acknowledge disability studies and feminist scholars in shaping our perspective in this article. At least one author identifes as autistic, while our projects have involved collaboration and consultation with autism charities and autistic people. All authors are based in UK higher education institutions as faculty and researchers. The latter is a limitation as we inevitably approach the topic with a Western, privileged perspective. The studies we discuss in this paper are also limited to English language publications in Western contexts. In addition, we had to limit the discussion to autism, omitting other neurodivergent identities such as ADHD.

While the defcit approaches to autistic communication are a point of contention, we evaluated current research approaches consistently and objectively, to the best of our ability. Our aim is to move the feld forward by critiquing the practices and values that may perpetuate discrimination and under-representation of autistic people in research while simultaneously drawing attention to the role of applied linguistics in helping us understand, and foreground, the positive experiences such as joy, excitement, and mutual understanding that focussed interests can bring.
